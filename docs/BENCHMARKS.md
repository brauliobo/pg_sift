# Benchmarks

Benchmarks of the PostgreSQL extension. The SQLite extension has its own, against FTS5: [sqlite/README.md](../sqlite/README.md#numbers-against-fts5).

**The final pass of 3.1.0** (October 2026): every number of the generated tables below and of the README was measured on pg_sift 3.1.0, the tag `v3.1.0` built from a `git archive` of the tag in a scratch directory (`SIFT_SRC=ref:v3.1.0 bench/bench final --size standard --docs`), on the host named in each table. Every engine had the same budget of 3 CPUs for its server: the server processes of each engine (every PostgreSQL lane and the Elasticsearch process) run in a systemd scope with `CPUQuota=300%` pinned to four physical cores, and the load generators (`pgbench`, `cell.py`, the Rust `xbench`) and the harness run outside the cap on the other four. PostgreSQL engines have 2 parallel workers (and 2 for an index build), Elasticsearch `node.processors` 3 and, by the rule of the store pass (one shard per CPU of the budget), 3 shards each merged to one segment: its top-k scores use the statistics of each shard, not of the whole index. The noise of a batch is judged by the host's other work in its window (`bench/steps/lib.sh`, no waiting between batches); the windows flagged are listed in the `noisy.txt` of each table's run directory, and each cell is the median of 2 to 3 interleaved rounds. The online-store tables ran in docker containers of 1 and 3 CPUs on the whole host. No figure of the 3.0.x passes is presented as current: the sections marked history below keep older sessions with their date and build.

**The 10,000,000-order `sift_table` check (history, 3.0.0 pass, not repeated for 3.1.0).** After the 3.0 pass, 10,000,000 orders were loaded into `sift_table` (and PostgreSQL's heap) and the ten questions were run on both, one timed run each, only to compare the answers. The load of the `sift_table` used transactions of 1,000,000 rows with a `VACUUM` after each: the single `COPY` of the harness's first attempt fails at line 4,059,072 with `Too many write buffers waiting for promotion`, a known issue of 3.0.0 (a transaction could write about 500 MB) fixed in 3.0.1 ([release notes](releases/v3.0.1.md)). All ten answers equalled PostgreSQL's (relative difference 0), including the two search questions with facets and the newest orders that failed in the earlier 10,000,000-order run; `count(*)` and `sum(amount)` of both tables were equal (10,000,000 rows, 2,296,518,589.40). The timings of that check are not published.

The suite has two tiers of the same engines, query classes and workloads (`bench/README.md`): **slim**, the default of `bench/bench all`, a smaller
corpus (about 10 minutes, 13 with the cold-cache tables), whose tables are the first section here, are generated from [results/](results/) by `bench/bench all --docs` and are the source of
every number in the README; and **full** (`--full`, every article of the dump and GeoNames cities500, about an hour), whose last session is kept below
as history: its tables are not regenerated by the slim tier, carry the date and the sift build they were measured on, and are redrawn only by a new
`bench/bench all --full --docs`. Compare engines within one table; **ratios depend on the size of the corpus and on what else the host runs**:
the slim ratios are close to the full ones for most classes and differ for some (the section "Slim against full" below says which).

The analytics sections (an online store, then updates and deletes) follow the slim tier; they measure the column engine against Elasticsearch and ClickHouse and are a separate suite (`bench/bench store`, `bench/README.md`).

## Slim tier (the default): 50,000 articles, 34,165 places

Simple English Wikipedia (50,000 articles, 8 clients) and GeoNames cities15000 (34,165 places, 1 client), every engine of `bench/engines.tsv`, the same query classes as the full tier and its workloads but the unordered scan, cells of at most 1 s that stop at a relative standard error of 5%, 2-3 interleaved rounds. Generated by `bench/bench all --docs` from [results/](results/) (tier `slim`); the README quotes these tables. Measured 2026-10-20 on AMD Ryzen 7 9700X (16 threads), PostgreSQL 18.6, sift 49cbea15f8d4, Elasticsearch 9.5.5-1.

## Analytics: an online store

The analytics benchmark for everyone: a table of orders and ten questions anyone can ask of it, on PostgreSQL, PostgreSQL with pg_sift, Citus (its columnar table access method, one node), Elasticsearch and ClickHouse, every server alone in a docker container with the same CPU and memory limits and the same seeded rows (the internal shapes of the column engine's own tests, `docs/COL-PLAN.md`, are not these queries). The table is `orders(id, customer_id, country, category, status, amount, quantity, created_at, title)`: 30 countries, 40 categories, 5 statuses, amounts of $2.99 to $1,499.95, three years of orders and a product title of two adjectives and a noun; the ten queries are the files of [bench/sql/store/queries/](../bench/sql/store/queries/). Generated from [results/store.json](results/store.json) by `bench/bench store all --docs`; `bench/bench store report` redraws the tables from the committed file without measuring.

**The final pass of 3.1.0 measured 1,000,000 orders only.** It was run by the store stage of `bench/bench final --size standard` (`bench/bench store all --sizes 1000000`, the preset `standard` of `bench/presets.env`: thread budgets of 1 and 3 CPUs, 5 timed runs after 2 untimed ones, 2 cold runs and 2 restart runs, updates at 0, 1,000 and 10,000 rows/s with 10-second windows per rate). Only the 3-CPU tables are published, the budget of the search tables; the 1-CPU raw cells are kept in `docs/results/store.json`, not published. 10,000,000 orders were not repeated for 3.1.0 (`--size full` does): the last 10,000,000 run (2026-10-18, a 3.0 development build, history) is in the git history of `docs/results/store.json`, and at that size ClickHouse was faster than `sift_col` on 7 of the 10 questions, so the ranking at 1,000,000 orders (the data fits in memory) must not be read as the ranking at ten times the size. `docs/results/store-1M-first-pass.json` is the first pass of the 3.0 session (history), with the earlier harness (a wait for a quiet host before every cell); it is kept as data, not published, and its Elasticsearch cells were measured on the one-shard index described below. The gap to ClickHouse and Elasticsearch per question, with the ratios and a profile of pg_sift, is in [BENCH-GAP.md](BENCH-GAP.md).

### Fair comparison: the same threads for every engine

The five servers start different numbers of threads on their own (PostgreSQL and Citus parallel workers only when the planner asks for them, ClickHouse one thread per CPU it sees plus a merge pool, Elasticsearch thread pools and a JVM with GC and JIT threads), so each is given the same thread budget N = 3 CPUs, the budget of the search tables, and the tables below are those of that budget (the harness also times 1 CPU, `bench/bench store --cpus-list 1,3`, the default; those cells are not published).

- **Quota, not pinning.** Every container has `--cpus N` (a CFS quota), the same `--memory` and `--memory-swap`; the CPUs are not pinned (no `--cpuset-cpus`, `taskset` or `AllowedCPUs`), which is what a hosted container gets and keeps the cache and memory the engines see alike.
- **Thread knobs.** PostgreSQL, pg_sift and Citus: `max_parallel_workers_per_gather` and `max_parallel_maintenance_workers` N - 1 (a leader and N - 1 workers are N processes), `max_worker_processes` and `max_parallel_workers` 16 and low parallel cost knobs, the same for the three servers, no forced parallel plans. Citus is columnar-only and one node: the table is in the `columnar` access method with the default chunk, stripe and compression settings, and whether its scan uses the workers is the planner's choice (the workers it planned for a `count(*)` are listed in the Configuration section). ClickHouse: `max_threads`, `max_insert_threads`, `max_final_threads` and `background_pool_size` N (merges and mutations run at most N tasks). Elasticsearch: `node.processors` N and `-XX:ActiveProcessorCount=N` in `ES_JAVA_OPTS`. Elasticsearch and ClickHouse are recreated on their data volumes to change budget; PostgreSQL reloads.
- **Verified, and recorded.** After every start and change of budget the servers are asked what they run with (`SHOW max_parallel_workers_per_gather`, `system.settings` / `system.server_settings`, `GET _nodes/os?filter_path=**.allocated_processors`, `docker inspect` for the quota and an empty cpuset) and the run stops on a difference; the values are stored with each result set and listed in the Configuration table below. A question that a system answers with an error is kept as a result: its cell is `n/a` and a note below the table says which question and why.
- **Same data, memory and warm-cache policy.** One seeded CSV (sha256 below), the same memory cap for every container, untimed runs before the timed ones, and the operating system's page cache never dropped (it needs root). The cold cells (3 CPUs only) are the first run after the server's memory was emptied: one restart of each server at the start of the stage, then before every run PostgreSQL and Citus evict every shared buffer (`pg_buffercache_evict_all`), ClickHouse drops its mark, uncompressed-block, primary-key and index caches, and every run uses a new connection. The check that this is as cold as a restart is printed under each cold table: the same question after real restarts against the emptied memory. Elasticsearch failed it clearly (the emptied index took 208 ms where a restart took 469 ms for the distinct customers per month: after a restart its JVM has not run the code paths of the question), so its cold cells restart the container for every run; PostgreSQL and ClickHouse differed in one of the two measurements of the pass (PostgreSQL 218 ms emptied against 250 ms restarted in the pass itself and 207 ms in the first pass, ClickHouse 10.5 against 16.5 and 11.5; the ClickHouse difference is above 20%), restarts themselves varying that much between passes, so their cold cells are the restart-based ones of the first pass (median of 3), as the table says. Sift, Citus and `sift_table` agreed within 12%.
- **Elasticsearch layout.** One shard is searched by one thread, whatever `node.processors` says, so an index of one shard (and one segment) cannot use three cores: measured on the same data, the container used 1.1 to 1.5 cores (of 3) for the revenue per month, the top categories and the distinct customers per month with one shard, 2.1 to 3.1 with three, and those questions took 18.2, 20.4 and 58.3 ms against 6.1, 6.8 and 32.3 ms. The index therefore has one shard per CPU of the budget the load runs at (3), each force-merged to one segment after the load. Because the top 10 of a terms aggregation over several shards is only exact when each shard returns its whole list, the category facets ask for `shard_size` 1000 (there are 40 categories); the first attempt with the default failed the equality check against PostgreSQL, as it should. Questions that are one lookup or a count of a few thousand documents are 0.3 to 1 ms and cannot use three cores in any layout. The cores a container used during an engine's own requests (its cgroup CPU over the wall time of those requests) are in `cpu_cores` of the results; a value above 1.05 times the quota is not recorded (the quota is enforced per 100 ms, so a request of a few milliseconds may burst above it). At 3 CPUs the medians over the questions were 2.3 for PostgreSQL (0.7 to 2.9), 1.0 for Citus (its columnar scan used no workers), 1.9 for ClickHouse (1.0 to 2.4), 2.3 for Elasticsearch (1.6 to 3.0; 8 of 10 questions: the others went above the quota in a burst), 0.85 for `sift_col` (0.72 to 0.95: its plans were serial on all ten questions) and 0.93 for `sift_table` (2.8 and 2.5 on the revenue per month and the distinct customers per month, where it used workers); at 1 CPU every system but Elasticsearch was at 0.85 to 1.0 on the median.
- **Interleaving costs every engine a little.** The engines of a question are timed in turn within each run, so the first request to an engine comes after another engine used the machine (its threads were idle, its caches were cold). Measured in one process on the same data, the median of 25 consecutive requests against the median of the interleaved ones: Elasticsearch 0.27 against 0.73 ms (one order by its id), 0.56 against 1.97 ms (orders per country), 0.40 against 1.78 ms (a filtered count); `sift_col` 0.20 against 0.72 ms and 0.50 against 1.40 ms; PostgreSQL 0.03 against 0.15 ms for the same lookup; ClickHouse 2.3 against 2.4 ms and 4.8 against 6.5 ms for the revenue per month. The interleaved method adds roughly 0.5 to 1.5 ms to cells below 5 ms for every engine, which is a larger share for the engines with the smallest cells; it is kept for 3.0 because it is the method of all the published tables, and a back-to-back variant is planned.
- **`count(DISTINCT customer_id) GROUP BY month` with 2 and 4 workers (recheck, 1,000,000 orders).** The scan is now priced by the bytes it reads, so the question was timed again with `max_parallel_workers_per_gather` 0, 2 and 4 on a container of workers + 1 CPUs (median of 7): 5.2 ms serial, 4.9 ms with 2 (the planner keeps the serial plan: no workers planned) and 7.8 ms with 4 (4 planned and launched, `SiftAgg` in all three). At this size four workers are slower than the serial scan (1.5x); the setup of a worker costs more than the 5 ms it could save. The question was not timed at 10,000,000 orders in this pass.
- **One engine at a time, idle background work, noise judged by the cell's own window.** One client sends one request at a time. A stage starts with one wait for the other containers to be under 2% of a CPU with no merge running (`docker stats`, ClickHouse `system.merges` and mutations, Elasticsearch merge stats, PostgreSQL autovacuum) and for a quiet host (`scripts/quiet.sh`), and nothing is waited for between cells. Instead every timed window (a question on all the engines, a cold restart and first run, the updates of one engine) reads the host's CPU from `/proc/stat` and the disk traffic of the docker volume from `/proc/diskstats`, subtracts the CPU of the five containers' cgroups, of this process tree and of the docker daemon, and is NOISY when what is left is above 0.25 core or 2 MiB/s over the other work the idle host had when the stage started (recorded in the results: `window_s`, `external_cpu`, `external_io`), or when a container that is not under test used more than 0.1 core. The pressure stall information is logged for information only, because a benchmark's own loads and restarts produce it. A NOISY cell is measured once more at the end of its stage, not waited on, and stays flagged if it is still NOISY; windows shorter than 0.5 s are recorded and not judged (the host's counters tick every 10 ms). This host is shared (other jobs use 2.5 to 9 cores of it and move by 0.3 to 1 core between seconds), so a threshold of a quarter of a core flags a good share of the cells at first; the flagged and re-measured cells of each part are listed in the Configuration section and in `meta.tiers.*.parts.*` of the results. The previous harness waited up to 60 s before every cell (about 24 s per warm cell, 53 to 87 s per cold cell); the cells now take 1 to 7 s.
- **Why 3.** Each engine uses the parallelism it has (a leader and two workers, three ClickHouse threads, three Elasticsearch processors) while the idle containers leave the host room for the one under test, and it is the budget every server has in the search tables.

## Size against ClickHouse (1,000,000 orders)

Byte counts only (they do not depend on the host; measured on 2026-10-18 and not repeated in the final pass, whose store loads give the same file sizes: `sift_table` 196 MiB with 150 free before the compaction of a `VACUUM`, `sift_col` 146 MiB for the table with its index, ClickHouse 38 MiB): the store dataset of 1,000,000 orders, `sift.col_sections` for pg_sift (section 2.10 of [docs/COL.md](COL.md)), `system.columns.data_compressed_bytes` for ClickHouse

The columns of the table are 12.6 MiB (0.43x of ClickHouse LZ4, 0.75x of `ZSTD(3)`, 0.85x of the tuned schema); the title dictionary (947,520 bytes as a codec string list, 4.4 MB plain) is in the `title` row. What is left of the 13.2 MiB file is the pre-aggregates
(0.12 MiB), the unit directories (0.10), the zones (0.03), page headers and the unused end of each stream (0.07), the segment headers and liveness pages (0.13) and the metapage, directory and write buffer (0.05).
The `sift_col` index over seven of the columns and the month (the lines of the compression table above) is 10.9 MiB: its seven columns take 9.6 MiB against 14.6 MiB for ClickHouse `LZ4` (its default), 10.4 MiB for `ZSTD(3)` and 9.3 MiB for the tuned schema,
and the month leaf, the pre-aggregates and the overhead are the rest. The entropy of the low-cardinality code columns (`status`, `quantity`, `category`) is where the tuned schema is ahead (about 3 percent of the total): the
codec packs codes at a fixed width and has no entropy coder; a `zstd` layer on them (`compression = zstd:3`) recovers part of it at a decode cost the default does not pay.

## What is native, what is emulated, what is NA

Every cell of every table and chart has a number except where a table says `error`: every workload can be expressed on every engine, natively or by an
emulation that is documented here, and the emulations are checked against an exact SQL oracle (Correctness, below). Two engines have no native query
for some places workloads (a typed prefix ranked by BM25; a field boost), and pgx-bm25 refuses wildcards shorter than three letters.

| engine | articles | places workload | how it runs |
|---|---|---|---|
| sift, sift `positions = off` | all | all | native (`fields = 'name', prefix_index = 3`; the variants differ only in the reloption) |
| pg_search | all | all | native (`edge_ngram(1, 15)` fields, a typed prefix is a term) |
| pg_textsearch | all (ranked AND, phrase, mixed, filter, join through the tsquery filter and standalone scores) | count, unordered scan | native: the prefix tsquery `'gra':* & 'ho':*` through its `bm25` index (a tsquery `@@` filter) |
| pg_textsearch | | top-20, boost, filtered top-20 | **emulation**: `bm25` indexes on stored columns of the edge n-grams of the words |
| rum | all | top-20, filtered, count, unordered scan | native: prefix tsquery, `<=>` ranking |
| rum | | boost | **emulation**: a second tsvector with weights |
| pgx-bm25 | all (`join` works at this size; it is an `error` cell at the 25k quick tier) | all words of 3 letters or more | native: `bm25_wildcard` per word, `bm25_boost` for the name field; the wildcard expansion limit is raised to 100000 (the default 1000 made `san*` an error at 236,262 places) |
| pgx-bm25 | | words of 1-2 letters | **emulation inside the same index**: two stored columns hold the 1-2 letter n-grams of the words of the two sections and the word is a whole term there; the extension refuses a wildcard under three letters |


The pg_textsearch emulation exists because it has no prefix scoring (a text search configuration has no edge n-grams) and no per-field
boost. The harness stores, next to the native index and in the same database, `ng_text` and `ng_name`, generated columns holding the
edge n-grams (1-15 letters) of every word of the all-fields text and of the name field (`"grand ho"` becomes `g gr gra gran grand h ho`), the
analysis of pg_search's `edge_ngram(1, 15)`, and a `bm25` index on each. A typed query is then whole terms: the matches are the places
whose `ng_text` matches `plainto_tsquery(typed)` (a derived table, `OFFSET 0`, through the index) and they are ordered by the BM25 score of
the distinct n-grams of the typed words (pg_search scores each n-gram once). The boost is the weighted sum of two bm25 scores,
`score(ng_text) + 3 * score(ng_name)`, the name counting three times like the other engines' `name:{...}^3`. The score is
written `(ng_text <@> query) * 1` so that the planner scores the matches standalone: a bare `ORDER BY ng_text <@> query` over the `@@`
scan takes the index scan's current score, which that scan does not compute, and returns the matches unranked. This is an exact
BM25 ranking of all the matches, so its cost follows the number of matches: it is a faithful emulation of what the engine can express,
not a tuned one, and it is the reason for the p1 and p2 latencies below. The extra columns and indexes are not in the index-size and
build-time charts (they show the native index; the emulation has its own table below).

rum has no field boost either. The emulation is a second stored tsvector, `setweight(name words, 'A') || setweight(other fields,
'B')`, with its own `rum_tsvector_ops` index, ranked with `<=>`; rum's weight classes are fixed (A 1.0, B 0.4) so the name counts 2.5 times, the
closest to the x3 of the other engines. rum's `<=>` is a proximity ranking, not BM25, so the weighted ranking agrees with the BM25
oracle about as much as its plain ranking does (Correctness).

Word positions make the index 2.5 times larger and the build 30% slower and decide phrases from the index: phrase, mixed, `count(*)` and the join
are 1.6-7.3 times faster; AND, OR and the filtered top-10 do not change beyond the noise of the cells (their ranges overlap). Without them a phrase

pg_search in every class.

How pg_textsearch is configured: a `bm25` index with `text_config = 'simple'` on the body (the analysis of the other engines), the
database `default_text_search_config` set to `simple` (its `@@` filter parses the column with it) and `enable_seqscan = off` in its
database, which the extension's README suggests for getting its index plans; adapter: `bench/engines/pg_textsearch.sh`. A ranked
OR is `ORDER BY body <@> to_bm25query(terms, 'docs_fts') LIMIT 10`, which the index answers with block-max WAND. It has no AND,
phrase or NOT inside that scan: those are the tsquery `@@` filter through the same index, and the extension does not combine
filter and ranking in one scan. Written as `WHERE body @@ q ORDER BY body <@> ... LIMIT 10` the planner chooses a sequential
scan that tokenizes every document (3 s per query at 25,000 articles), so the harness writes the AND, phrase, filter and join
queries as the extension's plan: `FROM (SELECT * FROM docs WHERE body @@ q OFFSET 0) docs ORDER BY (body <@> ...) * 1`, the filter
through the index and the standalone scores of its matches sorted. The `* 1` matters: without it the planner replaces the score by the
current score of the `@@` scan, which that scan does not compute, and the top-10 comes back in index order, not ranked (at the 150k tier, 53
of the first 200 AND queries have more than 10 matches and returned an arbitrary top-10). Scoring the matches ranks them and costs roughly
50-100 microseconds per match, which is why AND is 6 queries per second at the full tier. Without `enable_seqscan = off` the planner counts a
disjunction of common words with a sequential scan too: 0.25 queries per second for `count(*)` against 37.9 with it off (a separate full-tier
measurement of an earlier session, 8 clients, 8 s).
Correctness: its match sets and `count(*)` equal the tsvector oracle for 200 substrings in the and, or, phrase and not modes
(`bench/bench check oracle pg_textsearch 200`, PG parser tokens like gin and rum, 25,000 articles, earlier session); its top-10 overlap with pgx-bm25's BM25
is below (`check rank`, 200 substrings; a soft check: it scores the PG parser's tokens and a bag of terms).

How rum is configured: a stored `tsvector` column (`to_tsvector('simple', body)`, a generated column, so the heap grows by
437.7 MiB and filling it takes 19.0 s on top of the 14.9 s `CREATE INDEX`) with a `rum_tsvector_ops` index, the same column gin
would index; adapter: `bench/engines/rum.sh`. A query is `WHERE tsv @@ tsquery ORDER BY tsv <=> tsquery LIMIT 10`, rum's own
ranking operator (`<=>` is rum's ts_rank-style distance from the positions it stores, not BM25).
Correctness: its match sets and `count(*)` equal the tsvector oracle for 200 substrings in the and, or, phrase and not modes
(`bench/bench check oracle rum 200`, 25,000 articles, the PG parser's tokens like gin and pg_textsearch, earlier session).

Reading the numbers:

- sift is ahead of every other engine in every one of the 45 cells of this session. Against pg_search the ratio is 1.4x to 30x (median 10.9x; the closest cells are the unordered scans of the one- and two-letter prefixes, 1.4x and 1.6x,
  where the result is a large share of the corpus and both engines spend their time fetching rows); against rum 1.5x to 415x (median 28x; the closest are the whole-word cells, 1.5-1.8x: 0.31 against 0.17 ms); against pgx-bm25 4.7x to 639x (median 100x)
  and against the pg_textsearch emulation 5.0x to 9,700x (median 15x; it is faster than pg_search in 16 of the 45 cells, mostly filtered, multi-word and whole-word queries). The index is 2.8 times smaller than pg_search's (50.2 against 142.0 MiB) and builds 2.1 times faster (1.4 against 2.9 s); the default index is the
  smallest of the native indexes but `positions = off` (44.4 MiB) is smaller still.
- sift `positions = off` is not needed by prefix queries (none of these reaches a phrase): its latencies are 0.89x-1.30x of sift's cell by cell (median 1.0x), which is the noise of the session, and it costs 0.9x
  of the index.
- pg_textsearch (emulation) is behind pg_search on the top-20 in every class and by orders of magnitude on the short prefixes: top-20 1.0 s at one letter, 404 ms at two, 14.8 ms at three and 2.6-4.5 ms from four letters on (sift 0.16-0.27 ms). The exact BM25
  ranking scores every match, and a one-letter prefix matches a large share of the places. Its native `count(*)` and scan are the prefix tsquery: 455 ms for `count(*)` of a one-letter prefix, 0.8-9.7 ms for words and the multi-word classes.
- rum (native top-20, filtered, count, scan; emulated boost) is within 2-5 times of sift on whole-word and four-letter queries (whole-word top-20 0.31 against 0.17 ms, four letters 1.22 against 0.27 ms) and tens to hundreds of times slower on the multi-word and short-prefix classes:
  108 ms for a one-letter prefix (415 times sift's 0.26 ms), 50 ms for a word and a one-letter prefix. It is faster than pg_search in 12 of the 45 cells (whole words, four-letter prefixes, some filtered queries) but never faster than sift.
  Its index is 94.9 MiB (1.9 times sift's, 0.67 times pg_search's).
- pgx-bm25 is 4.7x-639x behind sift: 34 ms for a one-letter prefix, 72 ms for a three-letter prefix, 43 ms for four letters, 5 ms for a whole word (with the wildcard limit raised, see above); it is faster than pg_search in 1 of the 45 cells.

## Correctness

`bench/bench check oracle|rank ENGINE` checks the articles engines against a tsvector oracle (match sets, `count(*)`) and pgx-bm25's BM25
(soft top-10 overlap); `bench/bench check --dataset places [oracle|rank] [N]` checks the places engines against an exact oracle in SQL
(`bench/sql/places_oracle.sql`), N queries per class (30: 270 queries):

- match set: the places returned for a typed query must be those with every typed word as a prefix of a word of the all-fields text, with
  the engine's own idea of a word (pg_search's tokenizer, sift's accent folding, the PostgreSQL parser for rum and pg_textsearch's native
  index, alphanumeric runs for the n-gram text of the pg_textsearch emulation);
- top-20, boost, filtered top-20: BM25 over the edge n-grams (k1 = 1.2, b = 0.75, the typed words' distinct n-grams, boost = all-fields
  score + 3 x name score, ties at rank 20 accepted): the share of the returned places that the oracle accepts as a top-20, averaged over the queries
  (`agree`; `exact` counts the queries at 1.0), and the returned places that are not matches (`wrong`).

The sift row at 34,165 places is identical to the earlier session's (the wide-prefix fix changed nothing here, as the diagnosis below shows: the differences never came from prefix expansion). Per class at 34,165 places the match agreement is 1.000 everywhere except `a3`
0.867 (310 returned places in all that the oracle does not return), `p2` 0.999 and `p3` 0.998; at 236,262 places `a3` 0.933 (380), `p2` 0.999, the rest 1.000.

**The remaining match differences are not bugs; they are differences between sift's analyzer and the oracle** (diagnosed on the 34,165-place run, all 270 queries, by comparing sift's match set with the oracle's for every query):

1. **`a3` (0.867, 310 places): the oracle does not fold the typed words.** Four of the 30 `a3` queries have an accented typed word (`haessleholm skåne sweden`, `san méxico mexico`, ...). sift folds both the documents and the query (`skåne` finds `skane`, as `résumé` finds `resume`); the oracle folds
   the documents with `unaccent` and compares the typed word as typed, so it returns 0 places where sift returns 1 and 163. With the typed words unaccented the oracle agrees with sift on all 30 `a3` queries and on every other `a2`, `a2s`, `w1`, `x2` and `p4` query (agree 1.000).
2. **After folding the typed words, 38 of the 270 queries (the 1-3 letter prefixes `p1`-`p3`) still differ by 116 (query, place) pairs**: 114 places the oracle returns and sift does not, 2 the other way. All of them are word-segmentation or folding differences:
   - 87 pairs: the place has a modifier letter inside a word (U+02BB and U+02BC, the okina and apostrophe letters of transliterations such as `Tʻakanrok`). sift keeps it as a letter of the word (`tʻakanrok`), the oracle splits at it (`t`, `akanrok`) and finds a word starting with `a`.
   - 21 pairs: a zero-width non-joiner or joiner (U+200C, U+200D) inside a word (`jank‌pwr` in Persian and Indic names). sift joins the parts into one token (`jankpwr`), as the Unicode word-break rules do; the oracle splits there and finds the word `pwr`.
   - 6 pairs: IPA letters in transcriptions (`[ɕrudˈmjɛɕtɕɛ]`, `[auˈɡustuf]`) that PostgreSQL's `unaccent` maps to Latin letters (`ɕ` to `c`, `ɡ` to `g`) and sift's accent folding does not.
   - 2 pairs (the places sift returns and the oracle does not): `老橋鎮CDP`, a CJK run followed by Latin letters, which sift breaks into a word `cdp` and the oracle keeps as one alphanumeric run.
   Each of these is a choice of the analyzer (sift follows the Unicode word boundaries and folds with its own table); none is a wrong result of the prefix expansion, and none is in a class with more than the three shortest prefixes. I did not repeat the diagnosis at 236,262 places.

The ranked workloads differ for another reason: sift does not rank by n-grams. A typed prefix is expanded to the indexed words and scored as them (the max over the terms), so its top-20 is another ranking than the n-gram BM25 oracle's, most visibly on the 1-2 letter prefixes whose matches are a large share of the corpus
(34,165 places: `p1` 0.458, `p2` 0.628 for the top-20; at 236,262 places 0.392 and 0.432, boost 0.198 and 0.275); from four letters on it agrees at 0.72-0.89. **This is the one place where pg_search and the pg_textsearch emulation, which rank BM25 over the same n-grams as the oracle (0.97-0.99), agree better than sift.**
rum's `<=>` is a proximity ranking, not BM25: 0.23 at one letter, 0.93 on whole words (earlier session); its match sets are exact. The oracle itself is sound: pg_search agrees at 0.97-0.99 and the pg_textsearch emulation, which is the same computation in an extension, at 0.98 with all match sets exact.


## Method of the harness and its run time

`bench/bench run` is one pipeline for every engine and both datasets. The slim tables are measured as follows; the full-tier tables above were measured by an earlier version that differs in how the cells were
scheduled (a share of the machine for the engines), not in what is measured:

- **One engine at a time.** Every engine has a cluster of its own (a lane; Elasticsearch a server next to it). The lanes are built one after the other, so a build is timed alone, and then all stay up and idle.
  For each class, the engines run their cells one after the other, each alone on the host (the slim tables above: every server in a systemd scope with `CPUQuota=300%` pinned to four physical cores and the load generators outside it on the other four, as the top of this file says; without `SERVER_BUDGET` every CPU to itself), the same client counts for all (8 in the articles cells, one in the places cells), the same
  statements (`--random-seed=7`), and the order of the engines rotates every round (engine k first in round k). Why not all at once: engines side by side compete for cores, memory bandwidth and the turbo
  budget, so what one of them measures depends on what the others do at that moment, and equal slices of the machine are smaller than any server wants (a build or a parallel scan uses more than one core); alone,
  every engine sees the same machine, and the cost is wall time. What stays uncontrolled is the rest of the host: a timed batch is not waited for, its window is judged by the work of others in it (`sentinel_*` in `steps/lib.sh`: NOISY above 0.25 cores over the idle host's, listed in `noisy.txt`), the load average is recorded with every cell and printed with every table, the interleaved rounds spread a
  drift over the engines alike, and a busy host shows as wide standard errors. Without `SERVER_BUDGET` nothing limits or shares the CPUs and the load generators run on the same CPUs as the servers, as any client would.
- **Cache.** The built lanes (the database with its index, in a stopped data directory) are cached by a hash of the adapter, the extension binary, the corpus, the harness and PostgreSQL; the slim tier does not write the cache (its builds are quick), the full tier does.

The cell method below (stop at a relative standard error, batch means) is also newer than the full-tier tables above, which used a fixed short time per cell and the spread of the rounds as the standard error.

- **A cell and when it stops.** A cell is one class (articles: 8 clients, queries per second) or one workload on one class (places: one client, p50 and p95 of the transaction latency, which includes the lookup of the
  query text) on one engine in one round. It is not measured for a fixed time: `steps/cell.py` runs pgbench in chunks that log every transaction and stops once the cell has at least `--min-time` (0.25 s) and
  `--min-samples` transactions (3 for places, 1 per client for articles) **and** its relative standard error is at most `--rse` (5%), or after `--max-time` (slim 1 s; full: articles 2 s, places 1 s) whichever comes first.
  A fast cell therefore takes the minimum, a slow one the maximum. A chunk is a count of transactions per client (`pgbench -t`, the duration option takes whole seconds only) when it is short and a client runs
  many transactions in it, whole seconds (`-T`) when it is long or slow; the first transaction of every client of a short chunk is dropped (a backend that has just started pays for its caches in its first query), a cell has
  no other warm-up (the relations are prewarmed after the build), and the statements are seeded alike for every engine (chunk k uses `--random-seed=7+k`). When fewer than `1 / rse^2` = 400 queries fit in the maximum
  (the batch means below need that many for 5% on a trace of unit coefficient of variation) the cell is measured for the whole maximum in one chunk: no precision is within reach, so the time is not split into chunks that
  each end with idle clients waiting for the slowest statement. What remains of a slow cell is the statements still running when the time is up (several seconds for the engines with seconds per query).
- **Single-client cells (places) in one process.** `steps/cell.py batch` times every Postgres cell of a round in one process on connections it keeps open (libpq through ctypes; simple protocol, the same script
  as pgbench: the lookup of the query text, then the statement, the latency from the first statement sent to the last result, seeded alike for every engine), checking the stop rule every ~20 ms of work. A cell no longer
  pays a process start, a connect, a log file and a quiet wait (they were 0.5 s of every 0.9 s of the places rounds of the 3.0 pass). The noise check is the host's CPU and IO stall time (`/proc/pressure`, `some`) over the
  cell's own window, above `SIFT_QUIET_MAX` (10%) the cell is measured again after the batch and is listed in `noisy.txt` only if it is noisy twice. Outliers are not removed: p50 and p95 are order statistics. The throughput
  cells (articles, 8 clients) still use pgbench, whose spawn is a small part of a cell of a second or more. Validated against pgbench on one cluster, three statements of 0.03, 0.1 and 0.75 ms, five alternating
  repetitions each: the p50 of the batch driver is a constant 5-8 us above pgbench's (the ctypes calls of two statements per transaction), the same for every engine: 25% of a 0.03 ms statement, 6% of 0.1 ms, 1% of 0.75 ms. The
  fastest cell of the slim places tables is 0.096 ms (1% of the 168 cells below 0.1 ms, 13% below 0.2 ms), so the ratios between engines move by 6% at most and only for the fastest cells; numbers of the two drivers are not mixed
  in one table.
- **Standard error.** Within a round: queries per second by batch means (the transactions of each client are cut into consecutive batches of at least 5; the rate of a batch is the clients' transactions over their
  pooled seconds, so a few slow statements weigh what they last; the error is the standard deviation of the batch rates over the square root of their number, with at least 4 batches, or, with fewer, the standard error
  of the mean latency, sd / (mean sqrt(n)), whichever is larger); the p50 by order statistics, distribution free: half the distance between the n/2 - sqrt(n)/2 and n/2 + sqrt(n)/2 ranked latencies (the 68% interval
  of the median). The standard error of a cell is the larger of the sample standard deviation of its rounds over `sqrt(n)` and the error the rounds' own errors leave, `sqrt(sum se^2) / n`; the value is the median
  of the rounds. The within-round error matters: the rounds replay the same seeded statements, so the spread of the rounds does not show which statements of a heavy-tailed trace a cell happened to run, and the earlier
  standard errors (the spread alone) were too small for such cells (and for the slow engines, which run few statements) by a large factor. Tables show `median ± stderr (min-max)`, charts one standard error as an error bar,
  and `!` flags a relative standard error above 10%.
- **Rounds.** 2-3 (3 is the most, more runs with a warning): after the second round only the cells with a relative standard error above 5% are measured again, except the cells whose own error per round is so large that three
  rounds cannot bring them to 5% (a cell limited by time: a repetition costs as much as the first measurement and moves the error by a factor of 0.8 at best). `bench regress` runs 3 rounds.
- **Cold cache.** Per engine and class up to 12 queries, each after the heap, TOAST and index files of the table were evicted (per relation, `pg_buffercache_evict_relation`, and `posix_fadvise` unless the data directory
  is on tmpfs, where there is no cache other than the shared buffers), one engine after the other. At least 5 queries run, then only while the cell has used less than 3 s: a query of seconds (the pg_textsearch emulation of the
  one-letter ranked prefixes) is not run twelve times; `cold.tsv` has the count.
- **pgx-bm25** (`bm25`) runs in every table of a run. Articles: `and`, `or`, `phrase`, `mixed`, `count`, `filter` are native (jsonb queries through `@@@` / `&@@`); `join` works at the full tier (18 queries per second) and is an `error` cell at the 25k quick tier, where
  the extension refuses the plan of the jsonb `@@@` filter above a join (I did not investigate why the planner chooses differently); `fuzzy` is unsupported (NA). Places: `top`, `filter`, `count`, `scan` and the name boost (`bm25_boost`) are native for words of 3 letters or more
  (`bm25_wildcard`; the extension refuses a wildcard under 3 letters, and refuses one that expands to more than `bm25_native.wildcard_max_expansions` = 1000 terms, which `san*` does at 236,262 places: the adapter raises it to 100000, a fix made for this session); 1-2 letter words are emulated in the same
  index with stored edge n-gram columns, reported as part of its index.
- **Visibility map.** Every places database is a copy of a vacuumed template (`VACUUM (FREEZE, ANALYZE)` once, before the copies). Before this session only the per-engine `VACUUM` ran, and in a freshly loaded cluster its first run left 13-56% of the pages
  not all-visible, so `count(*)` of the first engine read the heap (sift measured 0.22-0.52 ms for a one-letter prefix next to 0.12 ms for the same index in the next database, cold 10 ms against 0.13 ms). The articles database is copied from a vacuumed template and was not affected (21,456 of 21,456 pages all-visible in every engine's database).


### Sizes, cached cells and the release pass

`bench/bench --size smoke|quick|standard|full` sets how much a pass measures (`bench/presets.env`; `bench/bench --list` prints the table): 50,000 / 200,000 / 1,000,000 / 10,000,000 orders and 10,000 / 25,000 / 50,000 / 218,058
articles, with runs, warm-ups, cold runs, update windows and rounds that shrink with the size. **The release pass is `standard`, `full` is opt-in.** Why: the same ten questions on the same systems at 1M and at 10M orders
(`docs/results/store.json`, 92 pairs of systems): 15 pairs change order between the two sizes and the ratio of a pair moves by a median of 1.2x to 5x per question (largest 21x), so a smaller size is not a faster `full`.
1M keeps the published ordering of the headline pairs (pg_sift against PostgreSQL, Citus, Elasticsearch and ClickHouse); `quick` and `smoke` are for the development loop and are never published (`--docs` is refused). `bench/bench store stable A.json B.json`
compares two sizes: exit status 1 when a headline pair flips by more than 1.5x or its ratio moves by more than 2x.

**Cached cells.** The cells of the engines that are not under test (Elasticsearch, ClickHouse, Citus) are kept in `$BENCH_STORE/store-cache` under a key made of the engine image, its settings and statements, the dataset hash, the thread
budgets, the runs and the host fingerprint (CPU, threads, memory, kernel, docker, governor). A pass measures only the pg_sift engines (and keeps PostgreSQL as the unmeasured reference of the result checks) unless `--remeasure-all`, `--remeasure E,E`,
`--no-cache`, or a key changed. A published table marks a cell read from the cache with its date and key; cells of another host fingerprint are refused, entries older than 30 days (`--cache-max-age`) and cells that were NOISY are never used.
The pg_sift lanes are never cached. Loaded data is kept in docker volumes named by the content key (rows, image, table definitions, load code), so a pass loads only what changed. The cold method is `--cold-runs N` (evict the server's memory,
the default) or `--restart-runs N` (restart the container, ClickHouse and Elasticsearch ~20 s each); the restart-against-evict check (`--cold-check`) is off unless asked and stored once per engine image.

**Engines at the same time.** `--parallel-engines N` runs the timed windows of independent engines concurrently, each in a docker cpuset of equal size on disjoint physical cores (the SMT sibling of a core is never given to another engine), the sets
rotating across runs so that no engine always gets the same cores; the external-noise sentinel and the equal-budget rules are unchanged. `bench/bench store interference` measures the same cell alone and while the others run. Result on this host (Ryzen 7 9700X, 8 cores, pg_sift `sift_col` as the target, ten questions, 41 timed requests each,
neighbours looping the same question on cores of their own): with three neighbours at 1 CPU each the median of a question changed by +2% to +113% (the middle question +18%, 9 of 10 above 5%) and the p95 by +11% to +84%; with one neighbour
at 3 CPUs the median changed by -5% to +50% (middle +8%, 9 of 10 above 5%) and the p95 by -9% to +108%. Pinning alone moves a median by -28% to +18% (middle about -1% to -4%). The neighbours' memory bandwidth and L3 traffic cost
more than the 5% that was the condition, so **the release pass keeps one engine at a time** (`--parallel-engines` defaults to 1); the option stays for development runs, whose numbers are not comparable with the published ones.

**Resume.** Every run has an id and a manifest (`bench/out/run-ID/.run`: command line, pid, state, per-stage state); `bench/bench resume [ID]` runs the unfinished run again and keeps everything that finished
with the same key: stages, store parts (`store-<set>-<part>.json`, key = code, host fingerprint, data hash and every setting of the command), search rounds (`round-N.done`, the rows of a round that was cut off are dropped) and the cached
cells. Result and state files are written whole or not at all (temp file, fsync, rename), a result of another key is never reused, and loads are restartable (a data volume counts as loaded only when its record, written last, exists;
otherwise it is dropped and loaded again). SIGINT and SIGTERM stop the stage's processes, record `interrupted` and exit 75 (resumable); a SIGKILL or a reboot leaves `running`, which the next start finds out by the pid and
marks `crashed`, removing the containers it started (`label sift-run=ID`); `bench/bench clean --run-id ID` removes only that run's containers and directory. A failed stage of `final` is recorded and the pass goes on unless
`--fail-fast`; `resume` runs only the stages that did not finish. Locks are `flock` locks, which a dead process does not hold. `bench/test/test_resume.sh` kills a synthetic run at random points and checks that the resumed results
equal an uninterrupted run (same files, no duplicate rows, no half-written file). Exit status: 0 done, 1 failed, 3 a result differs from PostgreSQL's, 4 over budget (`BUDGET_STRICT`), 75 interrupted.

**Utilization.** Every part of the store pass reports the wall time against the sum of its timed windows and the share of the time fewer than `--busy-cores` cores were busy; a part whose timed windows are below 70% of its wall prints `WARN`.
The 3.0 pass was mostly idle for reasons that are now removed or measured: a process, connection and log file per cell, a 2 s quiet wait per group, a restart of Elasticsearch (~18 s) per cold run, ClickHouse mutations waited for serially,
sleeps of 1 s in polls.

`bench/bench final [--size P] [--docs] [--dry-run]` is the release pass: the stages store, compression, stats, gap and search with a printed budget each, the total checked first (default 25 min), a finished stage skipped when its key
(code, host, settings) is unchanged, and the per-stage times at the end.

**Practice this follows.** Closed-loop single-client cells measure service time and are labelled as such; a throughput cell at a fixed arrival rate (open loop, latency from the intended send time) avoids coordinated omission
([Tene](https://groups.google.com/g/mechanical-sympathy/c/icNZJejUHfE/m/BfDekfBEs_sJ), [wrk2](https://github.com/giltene/wrk2)); the update tables already run at fixed rates. Cold and hot runs follow
[ClickBench](https://github.com/ClickHouse/ClickBench) (hot runs after a cache-populating run; a cold run after clearing the OS page cache and, in the current rules, restarting the server); the disclosure (hardware, versions, settings, dataset hash) is in
each result file's `meta`. Changes between two result files are reported with the bootstrap interval of the ratio of the medians and a Mann-Whitney test (`bench/bench compare A.json B.json`), the rank test being the most efficient at finding true
performance changes in [the comparison of change-detection methods](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.14256).

The store passes print the share of the wall that is timed windows (`utilization` in every part file, `WARN` below 70% for parts of a minute or more): standard, every server measured, no part below 70%; with the cache the

**Quick against standard.** `bench/bench store stable quick.json standard.json` on the same engines: the order of pg_sift against PostgreSQL, Citus, Elasticsearch and ClickHouse is the 1M one in all 40 cells (10 questions, 1 and 3 CPUs);
the only order flips (3 of 100 pairs) are `sift_table` against PostgreSQL on questions 03 and 10. The ratios are not: they drift by 1.8x to 14x (worst: point lookup and orders per country against Citus), so `quick` finds regressions and

cells take the sum of the engines' times:

| phase | articles (50,000) | places (34,165) |
|---|---:|---:|
| corpus restore and the index builds, one engine after the other (Elasticsearch with its warm-up included) | 72 s | 71 s |

The peak footprint of the session is 2.05 GiB (`bench du`, `bench/README.md`).

What was measured to get the cells and the setup faster, and what was not taken:

| idea | result |
|---|---|
| cache of built lanes | **taken** for the tiers whose builds take minutes (the ab articles builds took 33 s and 1 s the second time), with `--keep`; compressed (zstd), the lane directories of the experiments of the first version were 12 GB. |
| shorter cells and 2-3 rounds with stopping at a relative standard error of 5% | **taken** (it is the default of the tiers; slim has 2 rounds of 1 s). |
| prewarm after the build | **taken** (the first cell of a class no longer pays the reads). |
| fixed-seed sample of the trace per class (`--sample N`) | **rejected as the default, kept as an option.** The cells are limited by time, not by the number of statements, so a sample saves no time. It also changes the numbers: with 300 of the 1000 statements the heavy-tailed classes move. |
| cold-cache session at the same time as the warm ones | **rejected.** The evictions would perturb the warm cells; it runs after them. |
| engines at the same time on shares of the machine | **rejected** (it was the method of an earlier version of the harness): the cells of all engines took the time of one, but the absolute numbers of the fast engines depended on what the others ran (differences of up to 25% in a ratio against a run of one engine at a time) and a share is smaller than a server wants. |
| shorter Elasticsearch warm-up (1 s per class instead of 3 s) | **rejected.** It saved 14 s per articles session and lowered Elasticsearch's throughput by 5-25% (less warm JIT), so the warm-up stays at 3 s per class (`XWARM`). |

The regression check (`bench/bench regress`) runs sift and sift_ref one at a time, interleaved in 3 rounds, which is what makes a 5% difference visible against the standard errors: the control run of the same commit as reference
(`--ref` of the commit itself) gave 0 regressions in 18 cells, the run with `sift.simd = 'scalar'` applied to the new sift only (a deliberate slowdown) reported five regressions (0.68x-0.94x of the reference) and exit status 1
(both measured with the earlier method). The full-scale check against v1.1.0 is its own section above.

### Cutting the cells of the full tier (history, earlier method)

this was the method of the published numbers (one cluster, the engines interleaved cell by cell), so its cells could not overlap; the time was cut by measuring each cell only as long as it needs and by not building again. Measured on this host at
load average 8-14 from other sessions, on 2 server and 2 load generator threads, the same engines (six, with pgx-bm25), the same classes, variants and rounds. The reference is the earlier run of the old method, `-T 3` per articles cell and `-T 2` per places cell, 1 warm-up round,
3 rounds, load average 12-23:

| phase | before | now | what changed |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| articles full, cells | 842 s | 518 s | cells stop at 5% or at the cap, no warm-up round for the Postgres engines, the third round only for the cells that can still gain (10 s instead of 240 s) |
| places full, cells | 1644 s | 574 s | 819 cells of a fixed 2 s: 518 of the cell runs now take under 0.5 s (149 s in all), 186 run the 1-2 s of the cap (216 s) |
| both, cells | 2486 s (41 min) | 1092 s (18 min) | 2.3 times faster; the target of 15 min is not met on this host (below) |
| cold-cache tables | 76 s + 185 s | about 43 s + 89 s (projected from the quick tier, measured 14 s and 40 s against 25 s and 84 s) | one process per cold query, 5 to 12 queries and 3 s per cell |
| builds for the cold session | 105 s + 68 s | a few seconds | the cluster of the serial session is cached whole and restored |
| quick tier, articles / places cells | 171 s / 348 s | 125 s / 210 s | |

More than half of what is left of the articles time is the drain of pg_textsearch (the run used a 1 s cap; the default 2 s adds about a second to each slow cell): its six classes spend 298 of the 513 s of the cells, because the trace has statements of 8 to 12 s (`and`, `phrase`, `join`, `mixed`) that a `-T` window of 1-2 s lets finish.
That wall time cannot be cut without changing what is measured (a statement timeout or a cancel censors exactly the statements that decide the mean; pgbench ignores SIGINT until its transaction ends, and its log is only written at exit).
The cells of those classes have a relative standard error of 30-50% in a round whatever the time, which the tables show with `!`.

Validation, interleaved in one session so the host noise is common (quick tier, 3 rounds, the old `-T 1` cell and the new cell alternating): places 168 cells, 156 of them within 2 combined standard errors of the old value and 164 within 3 (largest 5.9, median 0.59,
mean ratio new/old 1.02, median relative difference 7%); articles 48 cells, 37 within 2 and 42 within 3 (largest 5.3, median 1.0). The large differences are all heavy-tailed cells (pg_textsearch, rum `filter` and `mixed`, sift_nopos `join` and `count`) where the old `-T 1`
value includes the drain of the statements in flight and is biased low (mean ratio 1.17), and its standard error, from fixed-seed rounds, ignores the sampling error of the statements. Against 3 seeds of 8 s per cell (the best estimate of the
truth), the new values of pg_textsearch `and` / `phrase` / `count` / `filter` were 77 / 47 / 176 / 252 qps (standard errors 13-47) against 39 / 32 / 114 / 115 and the old ones 36 / 22 / 164 / 160: inside two standard errors for the new, outside for the old; the fast engines
(sift, sift_nopos, pg_search) agree within a few percent with all three.

| idea | result |
|---|---|
| each cell until its relative standard error is 5% (batch means of the queries per second, order statistics of the p50), at most the cap | **taken.** |
| the first query of every client dropped from a cell with many queries per client | **taken.** A backend that has just started pays for its caches in it; the warm-up round is gone. |
| a cell that one more round cannot bring below 5% is not repeated | **taken** (the third round of 56% of the places cells was a repeat). |
| whole cluster of the serial session in the cache | **taken.** The cold session and a rerun do not build again. |
| stopping pgbench early (SIGINT, SIGTERM, `pg_cancel_backend`) | **rejected.** SIGINT is ignored until the transaction ends, a kill loses the log, a cancel drops the longest statements and so biases the mean. |
| `-T` / `-P` with fractions of a second | **rejected.** pgbench takes whole seconds; short chunks use `-t`. |
| a load generator of my own (Python or psql) | **rejected for the throughput cells** (it would distort the fast engines at 8 clients); **taken for the single-client latency cells** (`cell.py batch`, above), which pgbench spent most of its time starting. |
| cold cache in the session of the warm cells | **rejected** (the evictions would perturb the cells), the cache of the cluster made it unnecessary. |
