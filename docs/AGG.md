# Aggregate pushdown

How `Custom Scan (SiftAgg)` handles lookup joins, partitions, partial indexes, sub-selects, `ctid` ranges and distinct counts, and how the differential suite checks it.

## Joins to plans

The right side of a lookup join may be a table or a plan: a materialized CTE, a `VALUES` list, a function (`generate_series`), or a sub-select PostgreSQL keeps (`GROUP BY`, `LIMIT`, `DISTINCT`, a set operation, a ranked scan with `LIMIT`) in an inner, `LEFT`, `IN` or `NOT EXISTS` join. `UNION [ALL]` of sub-selects, a statement-level CTE's sub-select and a nested `WITH` count too, and so does the `WITH` of a derived table (a ranked top-N over an aggregate) while the derived table reads nothing of the queries around it; a CTE of it that several aggregates read is planned inline like the statement's own (the cheaper plan wins). A lateral sub-select that reads the row around it keeps the normal plan. Test `agg_nested_ctes`, fixtures `nf_nested_cte_*`, row J32.

PostgreSQL's plan of the right side is the child of the node (`EXPLAIN`: `Lookup Joins: s (subquery)`). The scan runs it once at start with the query's snapshot; its rows are the lookup table, so duplicate keys multiply rows and a NULL key matches nothing, as in the normal plan (`EXPLAIN ANALYZE`: `Lookup Rows Read`). A rescan replays the groups; prepared-statement parameters are read by the child plan as usual.

The normal plan stays when the right side reads a volatile function, the join has no condition (`ON true`) and is not lateral, the right side is estimated above `sift.agg_lookup_max_kb` (rows times width; for a lateral one, its rows per distinct input of the scanned table, together), or its plan (an `Append`) outputs only the relation's columns and a condition reads another. A scan with such a join runs without workers.

**Lateral right sides** that read the joined row (`LEFT JOIN LATERAL (SELECT .. LIMIT 1)`, `UNION [ALL]` arms, `generate_series`, `unnest` of an array column, a set-returning function) keep PostgreSQL's parameterized plan as the child. The scan runs it once per distinct value of the columns it reads (lookup keys of the table or another joined table's columns) and multiplies rows by what it returns, duplicates and NULL-extended rows included.

- A call on a document column that cannot raise (`jsonb_exists(doc, 'k')`, `doc ? 'k'`, `doc ->> 'k'`, `jsonb_typeof(doc)`) passes its value as the parameter if the index holds it as an expression column spelled like the query's. `jsonb_exists(doc, 'k')` gives key presence for any JSON root (a path leaf cannot tell a missing key from a JSON null).
- The scan runs the child for the tuples that reach its join, PostgreSQL's plan in another order. A child that can raise (a division, a text cast, a sub-select) is therefore joined only when the query joins nothing else (the scanned table's conditions come first in both plans); else the normal plan stays, as for a child that reads the whole document or a column of a query above the aggregate's own (a sub-select of an outer query: its value changes per row of that query).
- A `jsonb_each` member `r.value` reaches the child as the jsonb its stored float stands for (the number, or a JSON null when absent), so `CAST(r.value AS integer)` works; the cast runs only for members of rows that reach the child and raises PostgreSQL's own error (`integer out of range`). A document the index cannot hold as numbers (a string or object member) keeps the normal plan.
- A path declared `float8` carries no scale: a child reading the member otherwise (a cast to `numeric` or `boolean`, `r.value` itself, as text) would see `12.5` for `12.50`, so it keeps the normal plan (test `agg_child_member_value`).
- A lateral sub-select PostgreSQL does not pull up because its query is on the nullable side of an outer join (`dates LEFT JOIN cte`; below `RIGHT` or `FULL` the normal plan stays) is pulled up within its own query level by the planner hook (`agg/flat/`), which plans both ways and keeps the flat plan when it runs in the scan and costs less. A sub-select with a join of its own attaches through its leftmost table, as `(a JOIN jsonb_each(a.doc) r) LEFT JOIN m ON ..`.
- A `GROUP BY` over a `UNION ALL` of selects (`FROM (SELECT t0 AS ts, 1 AS q FROM events WHERE .. UNION ALL SELECT t1, -1 FROM events WHERE ..) ev GROUP BY date_bin(..., ev.ts, ...)`) is planned both ways like a pulled-up lateral: each arm grouped by the keys with the partial aggregates of its groups (`count`, `sum` of `smallint`, `integer`, `bigint` and `numeric`, `min`, `max`), added up above the union, and the plan with the arms that run in the scan grouped and the others as written when it costs less (`agg/flat/pushagg.c`). `avg`, `DISTINCT`, `FILTER`, an ordered aggregate, a float sum, `UNION` without `ALL`, an arm with `LIMIT`, `ORDER BY`, DISTINCT or an aggregate of its own, and a join next to the union keep the normal plan (test `agg_union_partial_agg`).

`LEFT JOIN LATERAL jsonb_each(doc) r ON r.key = 'k'` with a constant key is member `k` of each row (one member per key; a row without it stays, with NULL columns). The scan reads it at row level as `(doc ->> 'k')::float8` and unnests nothing. A correlated child reads `r.value` only through casts of jsonb to an integer or float (a missing member and a JSON null are the same NULL; a JSON null casts to NULL in PostgreSQL 18); before PostgreSQL 18, with another condition, or with `r.key` read, the normal plan stays.

`LEFT JOIN LATERAL` over `jsonb_to_recordset` or `jsonb_array_elements` of a list of the scanned row, `ON true` or with its own conditions (sub-select `WHERE`, `ON`), is answered too: the conditions say which elements exist, and a row none of whose elements passes (no list, empty, all failing) is one tuple with NULL element columns, found before the snapshot check so a row the snapshot does not see is no tuple. The normal plan stays for a `WHERE` above the join that PostgreSQL could not turn into an inner join (`p.n IS NULL` sees the NULL tuple), a lateral condition that may raise, a lateral reading the row's other lists, and other outer joins of an unnested JSON function; `sift.agg_left_lateral` off keeps it for all of them. Test `agg_left_lateral`, fixtures `j_left_lateral*`, fuzz family `leftlateral`.

Other rules:

- Predicate-only joins (inequalities, range containment) test every candidate row; overlapping and duplicate matches multiply the inputs.
- Lookup keys, grouping expressions and predicates may read earlier lookup outputs; duplicates and NULL-extended rows stay distinct inputs. Inner lookups are ordered by dependency within each join boundary; statement-level materialized CTEs can be referenced from nested queries.
- Aggregate `FILTER` predicates may read lookup columns and source values together. They run per surviving joined tuple after all joins; an excluded tuple never evaluates a filter expression that could raise.
- A condition PostgreSQL pushed into a sub-select (the run condition of a ranking window, `rn <= 3`) is enforced by its plan, which then does not output the column the condition read; the scan does not test it again (a column the query reads keeps its place and the condition is tested as usual). Test `agg_pushed_conditions`, fixtures `nf_pushed_*`, row J33.

Tests `agg_correlated_children`, `agg_correlated_fanout`, `agg_derived_correlation`, `agg_append_children`, `agg_raw_value_children`, `agg_lateral_flatten`, `agg_left_each`, `agg_left_unnest`, `agg_plan_tables`, `agg_child_predicates`, `agg_lookup_dependencies`, `agg_statement_cte_children`; fixtures `pt_*`.

## Aggregates over CASE

`sum`, `avg`, `min`, `max` and `count` of `CASE WHEN p THEN x [ELSE NULL] END`, `sum` of `CASE WHEN p THEN x ELSE 0 END`, `count` of `CASE WHEN p THEN x ELSE k END` (`k` not NULL), `min` and `max` (integers, float4, float8) of `CASE WHEN p THEN x ELSE k END` and `avg` (integers and numerics with any ELSE constant, float4 and float8 with `ELSE 0`) of `CASE WHEN p THEN x ELSE k END` are planned as the aggregate of `x` with a filter on `p` (`sift.agg_case_filter`, on by default): the scan computes `x` on its kernels under a test of `p`, where the CASE would be evaluated for every row on the generic path. The rows do not change: a sum with `ELSE 0` is still 0 for a group where `p` never holds, NULL for an empty input and for values that are all NULL (it is read as `CASE WHEN count(*) FILTER (WHERE p IS NOT TRUE) > 0 THEN coalesce(sum(x) FILTER (WHERE p), 0) ELSE sum(x) FILTER (WHERE p) END`). The other aggregates over an `ELSE` value are made of the aggregates of the rows where `p` holds and the number of rows where it does not (`n0 = count(*) FILTER (WHERE p IS NOT TRUE)`): `count` is `count(x) FILTER (WHERE p) + n0`, `min` is `CASE WHEN n0 > 0 THEN least(min(x) FILTER (WHERE p), k) ELSE min(x) FILTER (WHERE p) END` (`greatest` for `max`; `k` a constant of the type of `x`; not a numeric, as two equal numerics that differ in scale or sign tie in an order that depends on the rows, and not a float zero with a sign), and `avg` is `CASE WHEN n > 0 THEN (coalesce(sum(x) FILTER (WHERE p), 0)::numeric + k * n0) / n::numeric END` with `n = count(x) FILTER (WHERE p) + n0` and the same division of the same sum and count as the final function of `avg`, NULL for an empty input, `k` for a group where `p` never holds (a `k` with decimals is added only when a row gives it, as the scale of the sum is the largest of the values added). `avg` of a float8 is the same with the sum and the division in float8, and `avg` of a float4 with `sum(x::float8)` (`float4_accum` adds in float8), for `ELSE 0` only: `k * n0` is no sum of `k` (the rounding depends on where the `k` rows sit: 23.852380952380507 as written, 23.852380952380948 as `(sum + 0.1 * n0) / n` on 3,000 rows). `DISTINCT`, several `WHEN` branches, a sub-select, a volatile or set-returning function in the condition or the value stay as written. A statement the scan does not run keeps the plan of the CASE as written. `test/sql/agg_case_filter.sql` and `test/sql/agg_case_filter_else.sql` check the shapes against the plan without the scan.

## Join key filters and shared lookups

An inner or semi lookup join whose key is one integer (`int8` too) or date leaf of the index (`JOIN stores s ON s.id = o.tenant_id`, `IN (SELECT ..)`, `EXISTS`) needs a row of the table with that key. The table's keys, loaded at scan start with its own conditions applied, therefore become a scan conjunct `leaf IN (keys)`: a byte table over the key range (a hash table above 4 million values), with the same test, zone pruning and source filters as an `IN` list of constants. A unit whose zone misses the range is not read (`Units Pruned`); other rows are tested as a set before a tuple is made, where the join would resolve every tuple through the combo cache. A multi-column join adds one conjunct per integer key.

An `ORDER BY .. LIMIT` scan (Top-N) takes the same joins, and by a text key too (`text`, `varchar`, an inline or dictionary leaf): the keys of the loaded table that pass its own conditions become the exact conjunct `leaf = ANY(keys)` of the scan, evaluated once per distinct string of a unit like a constant list, so no join is left for the rows of the heap. An inner join needs a unique index on the key (a duplicate key would repeat the row); a semi join (`IN`, `EXISTS`) does not. An anti join by a text key, a column of the joined table in the select list and a nondeterministic collation keep the normal plan. 600,000 rows, 200 keys: `ORDER BY dim_a_id DESC, id LIMIT 10` takes 118 M instructions and 188 buffer accesses where the nested loop took 6,461 M and 1.8 M; test `agg_topn_join_text`.

- A join keyed by a column of that table (`JOIN ids ON ids.id = d.id` after `JOIN d ON d.id = f.d_id`, inner or semi, no condition on the scan's own columns) drops the rows of `d` it has no match for, so only the others' keys enter the conjunct; no key at all (empty selection) reads no unit. The chain is also probed from the scanned row's column inside the rows of a calendar `LEFT JOIN`: the keys of `B` are a conjunct there too, and a join no tuple of the conjuncts fails is not looked up per tuple.
- Two tables keyed by the same scanned column give one conjunct, the narrower: `IN (A)` with `A` within `IN (B)` is `IN (A)`, and the pass over the rows for `B` is not made.
- A table joined by the column that an `IN` list of the scan names (an inner or semi join, `EXISTS`; the scan's `= ANY` of constants or a parameter) is read by one scan of its btree on that column, with the list as the scan keys and the join's `column = value` conditions on the next columns of the index as further keys, while that costs less than the table read whole (a descent for each key, a heap fetch for each entry found and the pages visited: `sift_agg_keyed_ops` against `sift_agg_whole_ops`; the same price decides the executor's load and the planner's). A dependent lookup's keys (above) take the same path. A table read by conditions of its own (the semi-joined rows of one list, through its btree) is such a dependent too: a report of one list reads the parent table for the list's keys alone, where it classed or loaded the whole table (20,000,000 events by 7,600,000 parents, 800 keys: 548 ms to 39 ms). Test `agg_lookup_scoped`; off with `sift.agg_lookup_pushdown = off`. When the btree holds the columns the join reads (key columns or `INCLUDE`), the rows of an all-visible page come from the entries (`heap fetches` counts the others), and the index with the cheapest entries per key is the one used (`sift_agg_entry_ops`); `NOT EXISTS` and `LEFT JOIN` keyed by the `IN` list are priced like the inner and semi join. Test `agg_lookup_in_keyed`, bench cases `lookup_in_keyed`, `lookup_index_only`. Test `agg_lookup_in_keyed`, bench case `lookup_in_keyed`.
- Distinct counts of one unique key of a joined table under FILTERs of that table's own columns (`count(DISTINCT p.id)` and `count(DISTINCT p.id) FILTER (WHERE nullif(btrim(p.a), '') IS NOT NULL)`, the key being the join's) are planned also as one scan that groups by the outcome of each filter and counts the ids once, under a query that adds up the counts of the groups each filter keeps (the key fixes the row of the table, so an id is in one group of a group key). The other aggregates are computed in the scan for each group and added up. The planner keeps this plan where it costs less than the plan with one set of ids for each count. The answers are exact.
- A join to a large table by a btree column, or an `EXISTS`, `NOT EXISTS` or `IN` over one, where the scan's keys are not a list it names, is planned a second time as the lateral child that has the same rows (`JOIN LATERAL (SELECT .. FROM big WHERE k = x.k AND .. OFFSET 0) ON true`; `LIMIT 1` for the semi and anti joins), which the scan probes in the btree once for each distinct key it meets (`Lookup Joins: sift_probe`, agg/flat/probe.c). The plan with the probes is priced by what a probe examines (the entries of the key that the conditions of the index let through, from the index alone or the heap) and kept where it costs less than the table read whole, the keyed load and the ordinary plan; `sift.agg_probe_rewrite = off` plans the statement as written. The forms taken: inner and left joins (`JOIN`, or tables of the FROM list tied by the WHERE), `EXISTS`, `NOT EXISTS`, `IN`, and `NOT IN` where neither side can be NULL; a sub-select, lateral entry, whole-row Var or named join that reads the table is renumbered with it. Not taken: a system column of the table, a lateral entry beside a table of the FROM list (the table goes last in the list), a `NOT IN` that a NULL could change, a sub-select in the join's own condition. The table the scan reads is not probed, and the child of a probe has no bitmap scan (the scan walks an Index or Index Only Scan).
- A table loaded and not yet vacuumed or analyzed has `pg_class.reltuples` -1 and `relallvisible` 0: the rewrite takes the planner's own estimate of its rows (the pages and the row width, `estimate_rel_size`), so a bulk load plans the same as a vacuumed table, and the direct probe takes an integer key of another integer type and refuses an index entry its child's filter rejects before the heap is asked about it (the heap is asked for every entry of a page that is not all-visible: 200 keys of 67 entries, 13,399 fetches and 14,206 blocks, 698 after). The other reads of `pg_class` in the scan are not sizes taken as none: the epoch of a verdict (`verdict.c`) is a change detector (an ANALYZE or VACUUM moves it from -1), the text-key trial of a join (`init_table.c`) has a floor of 256 keys, the top-N group walk (`topn/gcond.c`) a default of 100 rows a page, and the reserve of a map (`lookup/load.c`) is skipped; `map_probe` (`lookup/load.c`) takes the planner's estimate of the rows too (it took `Max(reltuples, 1)`, which for a unique key of a table without statistics made the keyed read win by accident), and counts the entries of a few of the keys in the index where the statistics say nothing about the rows a key has (the keyed price, below).
- `EXPLAIN ANALYZE`: `Join Key Filters`; `sift.agg_join_filter = off` restores the plain join.
- When the key is a column a partitioned table is split by, the keys also drop partitions none can be in (`Partitions Removed`): a range or list bound by the key range and, with few keys, by each key; a hash level by the keys' remainders; no key reads no partition.
- Rows equal the normal plan's, and a tuple the join drops never reaches an expression (a conjunct on the fact rows that may raise runs on rows whose key the table holds, as in a plan that joins before filtering).
- A text key (`text`, `varchar`, a deterministic collation) of a scalar leaf, or of a text field of the elements of an array (`FROM t, jsonb_to_recordset(t.col->'items') AS o(sub text) JOIN keys ON keys.k = o.sub`), gives a source test instead: the table's distinct keys are tested on the encoded form of each unit, which prunes the units whose dictionary or prefix zone holds none and keeps the rows (for an element leaf, the rows with an element) that hold one. The strings of an inline unit that no key matches are never interned or hashed, and a dictionary unit looks up the keys, not every row. The list is tried when it has at most 65,536 keys and a sixteenth of the table's rows (256 where the rows are not known): the source looks each key up in each unit, and a longer list costs more than the join it saves; a test that keeps more than half of the rows of its first four units is dropped for the rest of the scan. `EXPLAIN ANALYZE`: `Join Text Filters`; the same switch turns it off. A key list wider than the join needs is safe (the join still drops the tuples without a match), so the rows are the same. A per-unit presence set for the strings of an element leaf was measured and not built: a unit can only be skipped when it holds none of the keys, and 10 keys that occur once each in 375,000 elements of 38 units leave 10 units (30.7 M instructions and 397 buffer accesses become 11.5 M and 134 when only those units are read), 200 keys leave 37 units and 10 frequent keys all of them. A bloom of 8 bits an element would be 2.3% of a 16 MB index, an exact directory of the strings about 11%, for a gain that only a handful of rare keys reach.
- No conjunct for `LEFT`, `RIGHT`, `FULL` and anti joins, element-level integer keys and top-N scans (a top-N takes only integer and date joins). A plan with a preserved side still gets the conjuncts of its inner and semi joins (a tuple failing one never matches a preserved row, and unmatched preserved rows remain the NULL rows of the groups).
- When the join returns no column of the table, the filter holds the keys and an inner join's keys are unique, tuples the filter passed are not looked up again: `Join Key Passes`.
- Keys spread over more than 4 million values also skip units whose zone holds none. Two sets on one leaf (an `IN` list or a dimension's keys, plus a join's keys) are met before the zones are tested: a unit is read when its zone holds a value of both (test `agg_zone_sets`).
- Cost keeps the units the keys' zones leave (the leaf's mean zone width is stored with the index; keys count as points).

Tests `agg_join_filter`, `agg_join_filter_text`, `agg_join_filter_elem_text`, `agg_join_key_types`, isolation spec `agg_join_filter`, fixtures `j_join_filter_*`, row J28.

A combo of one input (a join key, a one-leaf grouping expression) finds the entry of an id below 65,536 by array index; larger or negative ids and multi-input combos use the hash table. A combo whose inputs are columns of one earlier lookup's output row (and the one leaf that lookup is keyed by, as the month calendar of a period join) finds its entry by that row: the row names the leaf's value, so a tuple costs one array read.

**Sharing.** `UNION ALL` arms (or a CTE read several times) of one statement that join the same table with the same key, output columns and conditions read the same rows in one snapshot: the first node loads them into a statement context and the others reuse the table (`sift.agg_share_lookups`, `Shared Lookup Tables`). Arms with other columns or conditions, a plan as table, a preserved side, and parallel workers load their own. Test `agg_share_lookups`, isolation spec `agg_share_lookups`, fixture `j_share_arms`, row J29.

**Classes of keys.** A lookup table of one `int4` key whose other columns, as the query reads them, are integer, date, timestamp or boolean leaves of a `sift_col` index of it is not hashed row by row when its keys are unique and the tuples of values of those columns are few (at most 4,096, 2,048 by the statistics at plan time): the load gives each key the class of its tuple in an array over the keys (2 bytes a key, within `sift.agg_lookup_max_kb`), and the map keeps one row for each class. The scan resolves a tuple by the class of its key, so a combo makes an entry for each class (a few dozen), where it made one for each distinct key, with the hash insert and the evaluation of the expressions of its table (millions, for a fact table's parent). The columns that tell the classes apart can be tests of the table's columns (`FILTER (WHERE dd.a = 1)`): the load computes each test for every row of the table from the leaves of the columns it reads, and a table whose tests take three outcomes at most is classed by them, however many tuples of values the columns take; a test that may raise an error, a test in the ON clause of the table and a query with a preserving join are not computed at the load. An inner or left join with nothing else in its `ON` clause, whose combo reads the columns and not the key; a key out of line (negative, repeated, a span over eight times the rows) or a tuple past the 4,096th keeps the table whole, as does `sift.agg_lookup_columns = off`. The workers read the array from the shared memory like the rows; the table is kept for the next statement like any other (`sift.agg_lookup_cache_kb`, `sift.agg_lookup_shared_kb`), under its own entry: a join that reads the key as a column loads the rows. A key filter of the join is not built from it. `EXPLAIN ANALYZE`: `rows classed`. Test `agg_lookup_dense`, `agg_lookup_dense_kept`, `agg_lookup_tests`, bench cases `lookup_dense_classes`, `lookup_dense_kept`, `lookup_dense_tests`.

**Expressions of a lookup table.** An expression of the columns of an inner-joined table that a `sift_col` index of it holds as a column (`CREATE INDEX .. USING sift_col (id, presence(name, city, ..))`, the query writing the same call or operator) is one column of the lookup table: the load reads its leaf, where it read the columns the expression reads and the expression was evaluated for each distinct tuple of them (a presence mask of seven text columns of 7.6 million rows: 58 s of the original query, 0.25 s grouped by the mask). A table without the leaf evaluates the expression for each row loaded; a left join keeps the expression over its columns (it is not NULL for a row nothing matched). The expression may be a test or a conjunction of tests (`nullif(btrim(name), '') IS NOT NULL`, `lat IS NOT NULL AND lon IS NOT NULL` as a boolean leaf: the query's `IS NULL` of the same text, or `lat IS NULL OR lon IS NULL`, is its complement, on the scan's leaves as on the lookup table's), and the key of an inner join is the leaf it equals, so `count(DISTINCT parent.id)` of `parent.id = child.parent_id` reads no column of the table. A table with such a column is not kept in the caches below. The planner prices the classes of a column of the lookup table by the range and NULLs of its leaf in the index (a bound the index always knows) and by PostgreSQL's statistics where they say fewer, so a table that was never analyzed is taken as classes too; the groups of a key computed from those classes are no more than they.

**Types read from the leaves.** The columns of a lookup table that one `sift_col` index of it holds as whole leaves are read from them, not from the heap, when they are `boolean`, `int2`, `int4`, `int8`, `date`, `time`, `timestamp(tz)`, `float4`, `float8`, `numeric` of at most 18 digits, or a text type (the dictionary entry of a value is made into a text once for each unit); only the integer, date, time, timestamp and boolean ones tell classes apart. A column of another type (`uuid`, `interval`, `numeric` of more digits), a leaf that is a path or lossy, or columns spread over several indexes send the whole table to the heap: `EXPLAIN (VERBOSE)` prints `Lookup Heap Reason` with the column and the index, and `DEBUG1` logs it. Measured per row of a 100,000-row table joined from 1,000,000 rows, one backend, instructions (perf stat, the parent 100,000 rows less 25,000 over 75,000): leaves 2,250 (`int8`, `timestamp`, `float8`, `time`), 2,400 to 2,700 (`text`), 2,900 to 3,000 (`numeric`); heap 2,800 to 3,250; classes 147 to 173.

**Per-backend cache.** A backend keeps such a table's rows for its next statement (`sift.agg_lookup_cache_kb`, `Cached Lookup Tables`), since loading a mapping table of half a million rows is the fixed cost of every query joining it. Rows are kept under the table's conditions while every heap page is visible to all transactions (vacuumed) and has the LSN it had at load. A write in flight, committed or rolled back, a vacuum that rewrites a page and a table rewrite all force a reload. Never kept: a table with a page not yet visible to all (rows not vacuumed since a write), a non-constant condition, a preserved side, a plan or non-heap table, and parallel workers. Test `agg_lookup_cache`, isolation spec `agg_lookup_cache`.

**Cross-backend cache** (`sift.agg_lookup_shared_kb`, on by default; superuser; -1 is the smaller of `sift.agg_lookup_cache_kb` and 5% of `shared_buffers`, 0 turns it off). A backend that loads a table writes its rows, in the flat form of a parallel scan, into a dynamic shared memory segment and lists it in a directory of 64 entries reachable by name from every backend (the DSM registry: no restart, no preload). A backend that finds the key's entry with the pages it sees now (same LSNs, all visible, as above) attaches and reads in place, so a one-query connection pays the attach, not the load.

- The segment holds key and pages and they are compared in full; the directory only has hashes. Rows are per role, as in a backend's cache.
- A write moves the LSNs; the old state's entry goes when the key is kept again.
- Entries together take up to the setting (least recently used first; a table over half of it is not kept). A segment dropped from the directory stays until its readers finish. A crash restart discards registry and segments.
- A backend loading while others load the same table (cold start) loads for itself.

Test `agg_lookup_shared`, isolation spec `agg_lookup_shared`, TAP test `018_lookup_shared`.

**Dimension map** (`sift.agg_dimmap_kb`, 64 MB by default; superuser; 0 turns it off). The classes of the keys of a dimension (the table of **Classes of keys** above: an `int4` key, integer, date, timestamp or boolean columns, at most 4096 distinct tuples of them) are kept in a dynamic shared memory segment of their own (two bytes a key and a row for each class) by the first backend that reads them from the leaves of the dimension's `sift_col` index; every later statement of any backend reads them in place. Unlike the classes of the cross-backend cache, which are the table's only while every page of it is visible to every transaction, a map is valid by one of two rules:

- the dimension's index keeps a journal of its changed keys (a child `sift_col` index is connected to it, `WITH (connect = 'fk -> dim(key) carries a, b')`, the journal on the key the join is by): the map is stamped with the horizon of the snapshot it was read under (`sift_conn_take` before the leaves are read) and a statement reads it when the journal vouches for its snapshot, no row was inserted into the dimension since (`sift_conn_inserted_since`; an update inserts a version) and no key was changed in it (`sift_conn_changed`; the statement's own changes count, so a writer's map is its own and is not kept). A write to the dimension makes the next statement read the leaves again and keep a map under a later horizon: no vacuum is needed and the size of the table does not matter. Switching the connection off (`ALTER INDEX child SET (connect = 'off')`) changes the column layout of the child index: `REINDEX INDEX child` follows it, and the journal of the dimension and its stamp stay. A snapshot older than the horizon of the map (a `REPEATABLE READ` transaction that began before it) makes its own map, which is not kept;
- otherwise the pages of the table, as for the cross-backend cache: every page visible to every transaction, the LSN of each page the same before and after the read and when the map is used.

A map that does not fit half of the budget is not kept (the statement reads the classes itself); entries go least recently used first; a join with a computed column (a FILTER, CASE or group-key test of the dimension's columns: the lookup table computes it, `agg_lookup_tests`) or a key read as a column of the dimension is not a map. On 1,000,000 facts over 100,000 keys, the grouped count and sum by an attribute of 60 values takes 112 million instructions without the map and 91 with it when the dimension was written since its last vacuum, and 105 and 90 when it is vacuumed (the classes of the lookup caches; the three-column dimension of the bench case `dimmap_shared_classes` takes 76 either way); by 3,000 values 135 and 113, `count(DISTINCT)` of the dimension's key by 60 values 136 and 115; three FILTER aggregates over attributes take 286 either way (no map: tests are computed columns). `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` shows `Dimension Maps Shared` and `Dimension Maps Built`. Tests `agg_dimmap`, `agg_dimmap_conn`, `agg_dimmap_multi` (a child index connected to two dimensions: each has its own journal and maps), `agg_dimmap_types` (every kind of parent column: int2, int4, int8, date, timestamp and boolean are maps; text, numeric and an expression leaf are not, they are loaded or classed by the lookup table without a heap fetch), `agg_dimmap_fuzz` (random writes against the normal plan), isolation specs `agg_dimmap`, `agg_dimmap_conn`, TAP test `023_dimmap` (crash recovery, a budget too small, twelve sessions making one map, a standby).

**Semi/anti trees.** The right side of a semi or anti join may be a tree of joined tables (`IN (SELECT .. FROM a JOIN b ..)`, `EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM a JOIN b ON .. WHERE a.k = o.k)`, or the `coalesce` of the LEFT JOIN PostgreSQL makes of `p.id IN (SELECT k FROM (SELECT coalesce(a.k, b.k) AS k FROM a LEFT JOIN b ..) t)`). The tree is PostgreSQL's join of those tables; the scan runs its cheapest serial plan once at start, as for a sub-select, and looks keys up. The scan-side condition reads one table of the tree (the key); ON conditions on the tree's own columns are already in its plan. The normal plan stays for a tree that reads the query's other tables, has only a parameterized or parallel plan, or has more keys than `sift.agg_lookup_max_kb` allows, and for a key that is an expression of two tables of the tree straight under the scan (`o.k IN (SELECT coalesce(a.k, b.k) FROM a LEFT JOIN b ..)`; `from_shape`). Test `agg_semi_tree`, fixtures `j_semi_tree*`.

**Self-join elimination (PostgreSQL 18).** `WHERE id IN (SELECT id FROM t WHERE ..)` over the table's own unique key is merged by PostgreSQL 18 into one scan of the table that holds both conditions, and the conditions are no longer in the FROM tree the scan reads. The scan cannot tell which ones apply, so the query keeps the normal plan (`sift.explain_why`: slug `from_shape`, "PostgreSQL merged a joined table into another scan of the same table"); PostgreSQL 17 and 19 keep the join and the scan answers it. What the normal plan costs, measured once on PostgreSQL 18.6 (not from the records; `ev` of 300,000 rows with 50 keys, warm, 11 statements in one session, `perf stat` instructions:u of the server processes): the merged statement 258.3 M instructions per execution, the same statement written as one table with the condition (`WHERE amount > 500`) 6.0 M with the scan and 258.2 M without. Write the condition on the table, without the self `IN`, to get the scan.

## Outer joins that keep the other table

A `RIGHT` or `FULL JOIN` to a table, and a `LEFT JOIN` whose right side is the indexed table, aggregate over the table that keeps its rows (`EXPLAIN`: `Lookup Joins: stores (preserved)` or `(full)`). The scan reads that table at start and marks the rows the index's tuples match; when the index is read, unmatched rows become tuples of NULL leaves going through the same keys, combos and aggregates (`count(o.id)` adds 0, `count(*)` 1, `FILTER (WHERE o.id IS NULL)` is true). A `FULL JOIN` also keeps tuples with no match, with NULL columns. `ON` only decides matches: a condition on the table's own columns, a NULL key or a duplicate key leave rows unmatched or multiply tuples, as in the normal plan.

- The normal plan stays when a `WHERE` conjunct over only the indexed table must be evaluated after preservation.
- The indexed table may be partitioned: the preserved table is read whole at start, whichever partitions are read. The preserved or looked-up table may be partitioned too (its plan runs once at start). A `FULL JOIN` to a view or sub-select with conditions keeps the rows they leave out out of the join; a `FULL JOIN` to a view left of the scanned table stays plain.
- A nullable indexed input may contain ordinary lookup joins and expand one JSON object or array before joining the preserved side. Empty preserved buckets contribute one NULL fact input, NULL lookup columns and expressions included.
- `ON` conjuncts of such a `LEFT JOIN` that read only the indexed table (and rows it unnests) decide which of its rows can match, so they are scan conditions: an `EXISTS` over array elements (a sub-select PostgreSQL pulls up) or a `jsonb_path_exists` runs there, and so does an aggregate filter on a test computed inside the nullable input. The test of an absent input (`CASE WHEN absent THEN NULL ELSE x END`) is read as `x` only when `x` is not true for the NULL leaves an empty bucket contributes (comparisons, `IS NOT NULL`, `EXISTS`); `x IS NULL` keeps the normal plan.
- A scan with such a join runs without workers: one scan sees every row.
- A sub-select in the select list or the `HAVING` of a grouped query (`SELECT kind_id, count(*), (SELECT name FROM kinds k WHERE k.id = events.kind_id) FROM events GROUP BY kind_id`, `HAVING count(*) > (SELECT count(*) FROM members m WHERE m.kind_id = events.kind_id)`) is evaluated by the node that gives the groups, once for each group, with the group keys and the aggregates it reads as its parameters, as above PostgreSQL's aggregate (`EXPLAIN`: the `SubPlan` under the `Custom Scan (SiftAgg)`, the condition as its `Filter`). The sub-select runs the normal plan of its own table (an index lookup or a sequential scan for each group, whatever the planner chose), so a query whose cost is that sub-select costs what it did; one that reads a column that is not a group key or an aggregate (a `Var`, a window function) stays out of the scan (`output_expr`). The statement is planned without workers. Test `agg_output_subplan`, bench case `agg_output_subplan`.
- A `HAVING` condition `column IN (SELECT ..)` or `EXISTS (SELECT ..)` of a plain `GROUP BY` (`GROUP BY kind_id HAVING kind_id IN (SELECT id FROM kinds WHERE enabled)`, `HAVING EXISTS (SELECT 1 FROM kinds k WHERE k.id = events.kind_id AND ..)`) is a condition of the rows, since every row of a group has its key. PostgreSQL moves a `HAVING` condition to `WHERE` itself only without a sub-select (it keeps the sub-select above the aggregate, run for each group), so the planner hook plans the statement with the condition in `WHERE`, where the sub-select is a semi join the scan answers and the rows of the other keys are not grouped, and takes that plan without a comparison with the one that tests the groups: it read less in all of 40 measured cells (0.09 to 0.78 of the cycles, 300,000 rows, 100 to 200,000 groups, lists of 1% and 50% of the keys, with and without workers). It moves the shapes where that holds: an `IN` on the column as it is (or a `varchar` relabelled to `text`) whose sub-select does not read the query, whatever it holds (the planner makes a semi join of every uncorrelated `IN` in `WHERE`; in `HAVING` it is a sub-plan of the groups, hashed or, when the list does not fit `work_mem`, run for each group), and `EXISTS` of a sub-select without aggregate, grouping, limit, set operation or CTE, with no aggregate of the query in the condition and nothing volatile. `NOT IN` and `NOT EXISTS` (the rows form is slower in some cells and faster in others), an expression or a cast of the key (`amount::int IN (..)` is a function of the key that equal groups may not agree on), a correlated `IN`, a `HAVING` with grouping sets (a key is NULL in the rows of a super-aggregate) and one without `GROUP BY` (one group even for no row) keep the `HAVING` and the plan chosen by cost; the other conditions of a `HAVING` of several move on their own. `EXPLAIN` shows the join under the `Custom Scan (SiftAgg)`, no `Filter` above it. Test `agg_having_where`, bench case `agg_having_where`.
- A table `LEFT JOIN`ed to a join of two or more tables (`FROM kinds k CROSS JOIN regions r LEFT JOIN events e ON e.kind_id = k.id AND e.region = r.region GROUP BY k.name, r.region`: a row for every pair, zero for the pairs of no event) is not one preserved table, so the planner hook also plans it as `events GROUP BY kind_id, region` joined to the pairs, and keeps the cheaper plan. The groups are made in the scan (`EXPLAIN`: `Subquery Scan on e` over a `Custom Scan (SiftAgg)`); a pair's `count` is its group's (0 for a pair of none, `count(*)` counts the row of a pair of none once) and a `sum` of integers or numerics, `min` and `max` its group's (NULL for none). The join conditions are equalities of a column of the table with an expression of the other tables, the conditions over the table alone are conditions of the groups, the ones over the other tables alone stay in the join. A float `sum`, `avg`, `count(DISTINCT)`, an aggregate `FILTER`, a column of the table outside the aggregates and a `WHERE` on it keep the join as it is (`agg_left_partial`).

Fixtures `ps_*`, `j_dim_partitioned*`, `j_full_*`, `nf_*`, test `agg_nullable_filters`, rows J06, J07, J30, J31 of the coverage table.

An `EXISTS` over array elements may read a column of a joined table, or look each element up in a mapping table (`LEFT JOIN LATERAL (SELECT .. LIMIT 1) m ON true`, keyed by the element and a joined column) before testing the result. The lookup is PostgreSQL's plan, run once per distinct combination of the values it reads, and a row's answer is kept while its elements and joined values repeat. `NOT EXISTS`, `EXISTS` inside an aggregate `FILTER` and an expression that can raise keep the normal plan. Fixtures `ee_*`, row J34.

## Partitioned tables

A partitioned table is one source of `Custom Scan (SiftAgg)` when every partition left after PostgreSQL's plan-time pruning (at every level) is a `sift_table` or has a `sift_col` index, all of one kind, of the same layout: the same columns and expressions in order, the same `paths`, the same pre-aggregate options. Attribute numbers may differ (a dropped column), the order of the columns of a `sift_table` may not; sub-partitioned branches, default and unlogged partitions are partitions like the rest. The query is compiled once against the partitioned table's columns; the scan reads each partition's units (segment by superblock) one after the other, each with its own dictionaries, so groups, `DISTINCT` sets and parallel workers (which take units across all partitions) work as on one table, with the normal plan's result. `EXPLAIN` shows `Index: (partitions)` and `Partitions: a, b, ..`; cost is the sum of the partitions' pages and rows. Pre-aggregates answer per unit as on a plain table. Privileges and row security are checked on the partitioned table, as in PostgreSQL (a role without access to partitions reads through the parent).

**Per-partition finalization.** When the group key holds every column of the partition key (the column itself, not an expression) and the partitions are direct children, a partition's groups are final when its units end. A scan without workers and with a `count(DISTINCT)` (the aggregate whose per-group sets make the table large; other aggregates keep the single table) then reads a partition as one item, emits its finished groups (with integer sums and averages, counts, minima and maxima beside the sets) into the result tuples and restarts the group table. The table holds one partition's groups, not all, and does not spill at `work_mem`. `EXPLAIN`: `Groups Finished: per partition`; cost is that of one partition's table. The single table stays for a key without the partition key, a partition of a partition, an expression as partition key, grouping sets, `DISTINCT ON`, ordered-set aggregates, a scan with workers, and a float sum or average (its lanes add in an order partitions would change). Test `agg_part_final`.

**Workers split the groups.** A scan with workers splits the rows by default (each participant takes superblocks and builds a table of the groups it meets; the leader adds the tables). With many groups that costs the leader a merge of every table and each participant a table of nearly every group (1,000,000 rows, 100,000 groups: 19.8 ms alone, 34.0 ms with 4 workers). When the plan expects thousands of groups, the leader scans as a participant and a key of the group by is an integer, a date or a timestamp, the planner also prices the plan that splits the groups: each participant reads every unit and aggregates only the rows whose key hashes to its share, so the tables are disjoint and small and the leader only adds them to its own (`EXPLAIN`: `Groups Split By: <key>`). 3,000,000 rows, 100,000 groups: 40.6 ms alone, 59.7 ms with 4 workers splitting the rows, 35.2 ms splitting the groups; 300,000 groups: 99.3, 207.4 and 86.8 ms; 1,000,000 rows and 100,000 groups, where the split loses (19.8 against 23.8 ms alone), the plan stays in one process. The rows and the float sums are the same to the bit as with the rows split, and a worker that does not start leaves its share to the leader. These keep the rows split: a text key (every participant would hash the string of every row), a `bigint` key (a numeric key), a float key, an expression, grouping sets, a two-level query, a top-N, a search, a correlated join, a partitioned table, unnested elements and a scan whose units are answered from pre-aggregates. Test `agg_par_split`.

The normal plan stays when:

- a partition has no `sift_col` index or one with other columns or options, is a foreign table, partitions are of both kinds (`sift_table` and heap with an index), is read with `ONLY`, or the table is an inheritance parent (a mixed tree too);
- a parameter is compared with a hash-level column other than by an equality of the column's type (`k > $1`, a value of another type, an expression key: the normal plan prunes the remainders);
- the scan reads one index for the shape (a ranked search with `sift.score`, a text search, a time series of unions).

**Run-time pruning and limits**

- A condition on a partition-key column whose operand is a generic-plan parameter or a stable function (`d >= $1`, `k = ANY ($1)`, `ts >= now() - interval '30 days'`) prunes at plan start as in PostgreSQL: the scan evaluates the operand at start and skips partitions whose bounds it refutes (`EXPLAIN ANALYZE`: `Partitions Removed`); a default partition stays unless the others' bounds refute it.
- A hash level is pruned by remainders: an equality or `= ANY` on every column of its key (parameter, stable function or constant) gives the values, the key's hash function their remainders, and a partition none of them hits is not read (`k = $1`, `k = ANY ($1)`, `a = $1 AND b = $2`; a NULL value or empty list reads no partition). Remainders are computed once per level for the key values (up to 1,048,576 combinations), not per partition.
- A hash level's partitions are found from the remainders by the bounds PostgreSQL keeps (one lookup for an equality, as a range key is bisected), and the value of an enclosing query's row (`LATERAL .. WHERE e.k = d.id`) prunes the partitions of each probe. A top-N that finds one partition left walks that partition's order array and fetches the rows from its heap (`Global Order`); more than one, or a partition whose columns have other attribute numbers than the first's, reads the units.
- A plan reads up to 32,768 partitions. The planner reads each index's layout, statistics and pre-aggregates once and keeps them in the backend (a few kB each) until the index's directory, its table's pages or visibility map, or the catalog entry of the index or table change. A table with more partitions, before run-time pruning, keeps the normal plan and `sift.explain_why` names `partition_count` with the number.
- The scan opens only the partitions pruning leaves (the whole query over 5,000 partitions of 6 rows each runs 312 million instructions, the normal plan 300 million) and releases one's pages before reading the next, so a statement pruning thousands of partitions to a few opens only those (range-key bounds are bisected, not proved per partition).
- PostgreSQL takes two relation locks per partition its plan keeps (heap and index), and the scan's plan takes no more: `max_locks_per_transaction` needs about 2 x the plan's partitions whichever plan runs (the default 64 x `max_connections` covers about 3,200).
- A plan is invalidated when a partition or its index is attached, detached or dropped. `CREATE INDEX` on the parent makes one per partition (also for partitions attached later).

A top-N of a partitioned table (`ORDER BY .. LIMIT`, docs/COL-SEARCH.md) keeps its best rows with their partition (number in the high bits of the tie-break key, TID in the low ones), reads the zones of all partitions' units as one range of superblocks, and fetches kept rows from their partition's heap by the partitioned table's column names. An unindexed table written first in `FROM` is joined to the partitioned table the scan reads (inner joins). Fixtures `pa_*`, `topn_part_*`, `t_partitioned`, test `agg_partitions`.

## Partial indexes

A `sift_col` index with a `WHERE` predicate holds only rows passing it, so the planner takes it only when the query proves the predicate (PostgreSQL's `predOK`: the table's conditions imply it, as for a partial btree or BM25 index). A query that may need a row outside the predicate keeps the normal plan, as does a generic plan of a prepared statement whose parameter the proof needs. Rows an `UPDATE` moves in or out of the predicate are inserted into or deleted from the index like any change. Among usable indexes the cheaper wins (a partial one is smaller; likewise an index cut by a key (`key_cut`) against one cut by block range), whatever the creation order. Every index that gives a plan is planned and priced, about one plan of a single-index table (0.05 to 0.1 ms) per extra index. Fixtures `pi_*`, `t_partial`, test `agg_partial_index`.

## Uncorrelated sub-selects

`col NOT IN (SELECT ...)`, and `col IN (SELECT ...)` under `OR` or `NOT`, are hashed `SubPlan`s in PostgreSQL (no semi join is possible); `Custom Scan (SiftAgg)` takes them as a lookup table over the leaf. PostgreSQL's own `SubPlan` answers once per distinct column value, so the NULL rule of `IN`/`NOT IN` is PostgreSQL's (one NULL in the sub-select makes every `NOT IN` NULL, no row; an empty sub-select makes it true for a NULL value too), and the test composes with `AND`, `OR`, `NOT`, `CASE` and `FILTER`. The sub-select runs once at scan start with the query's snapshot; `EXPLAIN` shows a `SubPlan` under the node. An integer column whose values span more than 4 million (statistics may not show it) is tested row by row instead of through a table. A rescan keeps the answers; prepared-statement parameters are read by the sub-select as usual.

No lookup table is built for a test of two columns (`(a, b) NOT IN (SELECT x, y ...)`), of a column the table does not hold (an `int8` column), or inside an expression that reads the rows of an outer join's nullable input (a group key `CASE WHEN country IN (SELECT ..) THEN jsonb_build_object(..) END`): the scan asks the same `SubPlan` for each tuple that reaches it.

The normal plan stays when the sub-select is correlated or reads an outer-query parameter, is not hashed (too large for `work_mem` and `hash_mem_multiplier`), reads a volatile function, locks rows (`FOR UPDATE`), or holds a sub-select of its own or an expression that may raise (a division, a cast through text). A scan with such a test runs without parallel workers: the leader runs the sub-plans. Fixtures `t_not_in_subquery*`, `t_in_subquery_or`, tests `agg_subplan_lut`, `agg_semi_lut`, `agg_nullable_input_keys`.

## ctid ranges

`ctid` compared with a constant TID (`=`, `<`, `<=`, `>`, `>=`, either side, `BETWEEN`) is a scan predicate when it is a top-level `WHERE` conjunct, on a `sift_col` or `sift_table`. The conjuncts make one half-open TID range (keys `block << 16 | offset`, in PostgreSQL's TID order). The block part is a range of superblocks (256 pages, or about 12000 rows of a `sift_table`): units outside are not read (`Units:` in `EXPLAIN ANALYZE`), units inside are read whole, and rows of the first and last block are tested one by one. A heap index stores the TID of a HOT chain's root while `ctid` is the visible version's, so those rows are looked up in the heap through the snapshot (HOT keeps versions in their page, so other blocks need no lookup). An empty or crossed range, or one beyond the table's end, reads no unit; an invalid TID constant is PostgreSQL's error. Cost keeps the fraction of the range's pages.

The normal plan stays for `<>`, `ctid` in an expression, in `GROUP BY` or compared with a column, an `OR`, `IN`/`= ANY`, a generic-plan parameter (a custom plan folds it), a partitioned table (TIDs are per partition) and top-N shapes (their probes read zones outside the range). PostgreSQL plans a tid range scan for a narrow range (`enable_tidscan = off` shows the scan). Fixtures `tr_*`, test `agg_tid_range`.

## json_build_object above the scan

A statement that reads a table with a `sift_col` index and builds a JSON object per output row (`json_build_object` in the select list of a window, a sub-select, a CTE or the top level) calls `sift.json_build_object` where it names the core function: the planner hook changes the call in the query tree it plans, so `EXPLAIN (VERBOSE)` shows `sift.json_build_object`. The text is the core function's (test `json_build_object` compares both byte for byte for every type of argument, key and error). What changes is that the category and the output function of each argument are looked up once for the call site, not for every argument of every row: `json_categorize_type` and the type cache were 5% to 13% of the instructions of five grouped statements over 300,000 rows that build 5 to 12 objects a row above 10,000 to 60,000 groups, and the statements run 4.9% to 16.3% fewer instructions (a grouped object of six arguments over 60,000 groups: 1,836 to 1,558 million; a nested object with `lag()` over a window: 2,130 to 1,766 million).

A call with no argument, `jsonb_build_object`, a statement that reads no table with a column index, and a role that may not execute `sift.json_build_object` keep the core function. `sift.enable_agg = off` stops the change with the rest of the hook; there is no setting for it alone. Test `agg_json_swap`.

## Memory of the group table

The planner prices a scan's group table (expected groups times bytes per group, about 185 for one key and five aggregates) against `hash_mem`; past it the scan is charged the spill, and the arm keeps the normal plan with slug `group_memory` when that makes it dearer.

The executor holds the same budget. When the table of an aggregate made of `count`, `sum`, `avg`, `min` and `max` passes it at the end of a unit, the groups go to disk hash partitions as partial blobs (the form workers send the leader); partitions merge one by one at the end, each split again by another hash while it does not fit, up to `group_spill_depth` levels (`group_spill_parts` partitions at most). A scan that must spill runs without workers. Float sums add in partition-merge order, so last digits can differ from an unbounded table's.

- `count(DISTINCT)` and distinct `sum`/`avg` of numbers spill with their sets, united when partitions merge. Each set is walked once for all partitions of a spill, and a `count(DISTINCT)` whose bitmaps held less than a value per 128 bytes of chunks at spill time starts the next tables as a hash set.
- The set of a column whose values fall in a window of at most 512 (`dist_narrow_words` words of 64 bits) keeps a few words per group instead of a directory and a 1,024-byte chunk, becoming a bitmap when a value falls outside; an integer column whose zones span more than the window starts as a bitmap.
- **Passes.** A serial scan whose table first passes hash memory (not in the order of its group keys, not with pre-aggregates, a join with parameters, a float sum whose order matters, or workers) starts again as passes over the units (`agg/spill/pass.c`): the keys of the first group column that is an integer, a date, a timestamp or a text are cut into 32 shares by hash, and a pass reads every unit for some of them (the rows of the other groups are dropped before they reach the table, as the participants of a parallel scan split the groups). The first pass takes the shares the table fitted in by the groups the planner expects and the memory used when it first passed hash memory, each next pass as many as the memory of the last held; a pass whose table passes it again is read again with fewer shares, and when one share does not fit the shares left are read together and spill as above (the same temporary blocks as the spill, up to 6% more instructions for the pass that was stopped); a table the estimate puts above about 25 times hash memory is spilled from the start. The groups of a pass are final when it ends: they go to the tuples of the result at once, with no partial state written or merged, so a `count(DISTINCT)` over a million pairs that wrote 45 MB of temporary files at 4 MB of `work_mem` writes none. `Group Passes` in `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` says how many; there is no setting.
- A scan that gives its groups in an order (`ORDER BY` the group keys) spills in ranges of the first key when it is an integer, a date or a timestamp, merged in that order (docs/COL-PLAN.md, "Groups in the order the parent sorts by"); another first key keeps the scan until the table is `group_nospill_factor` times hash memory.
- A scan of that kind whose first key is the value of a leaf or a `date_trunc` of a timestamp leaf, over superblocks whose zones leave most groups final before the end (`agg/spill/stream.c`), does not spill: it reads the superblocks in the order of the zones of the key (the lowest place in the order of the result that a group of each can have; a superblock with rows of a write buffer has no zone and is read first), and after a superblock that leaves the table past hash memory the groups below the zone of the next one are sorted and returned as a batch (`Group Stream Batches`), the others dumped and loaded again for the next. A NULL key is at the end of the order, and with NULLs first no group is final before the superblocks that may hold a NULL are done. When no group is final and the table keeps growing (the estimate from the zones was wrong), the rest of the scan spills as above, after the batches it has given. A float sum, a `count(DISTINCT)`, a join, an arm or a match set keep the spill. Test `agg_group_stream`.
- Shapes that cannot spill (arms of a two-level aggregate, grouping sets, set keys, top-N, ordered-set and generic aggregates, and `sum`/`min`/`max` over combos such as numeric and text columns) keep the scan until the table is `group_nospill_factor` times hash memory (8 MB at least), then the normal plan.

Tests `agg_distinct_narrow`, `agg_group_memory`, `agg_group_passes`, `agg_group_spill`, `agg_group_spill_fuzz`, `agg_group_spill_limits`, `agg_group_spill_order`.

## DISTINCT ON

`SELECT DISTINCT ON (keys) ... ORDER BY keys, sort` is a group by the ON columns keeping the first row's columns per `ORDER BY`. Each such column is a "first row" aggregate (`first_row(x ORDER BY ...)` in `EXPLAIN`), not the `array_agg(...)[1]` rewrite (which sorts the groups' rows): it keeps each group's best row, compared with the `ORDER BY`'s operator, collation and `NULLS FIRST/LAST`. The query's ORDER BY, LIMIT and OFFSET run above it. With workers each keeps its best rows and the leader merges them with the same comparison; several select-list columns share one comparison per row.

Rows of a group may tie in the `ORDER BY`; SQL leaves the row DISTINCT ON keeps unspecified, so the scan keeps the first one it meets, the same row for every select-list column (with workers, the leader keeps the better best row of the group and, on a tie, its own). The scan is planned for at most 4 sort columns after the ON columns; a plan with a unique column in the ORDER BY gives the same row as every other plan.

`array_agg(x ORDER BY o)` that the query reads only through a slice `a[1:n]` or an element `a[n]` of a constant n (in the select list, the sort and the HAVING, up to 1,000 rows; read whole anywhere it is the sort of the group's rows) keeps the n best rows of each group in memory as the rows pass and gives them, in order, to the aggregate's own functions when the scan has ended (`EXPLAIN` shows `array_agg(x ORDER BY o)[1:n]`): a row that comes after the group's n-th is dropped after the compare of its sort keys, and the others are read and placed by a binary search, after the rows they tie with. Every `array_agg` of the slice has its own rows. The groups' rows are held until the scan ends, as the DISTINCT ON rows are, so a query whose groups would not fit the memory of a hash aggregate is refused (`first_memory`). `row_number() OVER (PARTITION BY k ORDER BY o) <= N` (N > 1) is written this way before it is planned (`agg/latest.c`). `rank()` and `dense_rank()` `<= N` (N up to 1,000) are written with the arrays not cut and a mark on each `array_agg` that only the scan reads (`EXPLAIN` shows `[rank <= N]` or `[dense_rank <= N]`): a group keeps the rows of rank <= N (the N best and the rows that tie with the N-th) or of the N best values of the sort keys, and the window function and its condition run above them, so the ties are the window's own. A group whose rows all tie keeps them all.

The normal plan stays when a sort or select-list expression is volatile, a value is an array or a joined table's column, or estimated groups times bytes of best rows exceed hash-aggregate memory (`work_mem` times `hash_mem_multiplier`): groups are not spilled. A volatile select-list function PostgreSQL evaluates after the rows are picked does not matter. Fixtures `do_*`, `t_distinct_on`, `pa_distinct_on`, test `agg_distinct_on`.

## Counting distinct values over the rows of a scan

A report that aggregates per-row aggregates (`GROUP BY id, key`, a `UNION ALL` of such arms) and counts `count(DISTINCT id)` per group above them reads their rows through a join (a spine of ranges) and a sort of every row on group columns and id. When the plan reads `Custom Scan (SiftAgg)` nodes and an aggregate counts distinct `int2`, `int4` or `int8` values, `Custom Scan (SiftAggRows)` takes the rows instead:

- a hash table of groups (the first spelling of a key is shown) with `count`, `sum` and `avg` per group (PostgreSQL's own transition and final functions: same types and errors);
- per `count(DISTINCT x)` argument, one set of (group, value) pairs with a bit for every aggregate of that argument that took the row, so `FILTER`s share it. The set is bounded by `hash_mem`: bitmaps while the values are dense in their range, a hash set when they are spread over it (a bitmap that costs over 128 bytes a value is given up for one), and once the hash set holds 262,144 pairs, or `hash_mem` is full, the pairs are sorted as 8-byte keys (an array of `hash_mem`, sorted by radix, with sorted runs on disk past it; a set past the cache misses on every pair, and the sort of 20 million pairs took half the time of the hash set in the measurement; the array is stripped of its repeats once at a million pairs and, when they were a quarter of it, each time it fills, so a column of repeats does not grow it to `hash_mem`; there is no switch: `hash_mem` bounds the sort as before) and equal pairs are counted at the end (`Distinct Sets Spilled` in `EXPLAIN ANALYZE`).

The child plan's projection evaluates group expressions, arguments and `FILTER`s, so any expression is allowed. Grouping sets, ordered or `DISTINCT` aggregates other than `count`, and aggregates other than `count`, `sum`, `avg` keep the normal plan. Float sums and averages add in arrival order (the sort adds in id order), so last digits can differ, as between the scan's aggregates and the normal plan's. `sift.agg_rows = off` restores the sort.

**Streaming `count(DISTINCT x)` of an integer column.** When a `count(DISTINCT x)` scan's sets would outgrow `hash_mem` and the column's superblocks hold `x` ranges that overlap little (a column growing with the heap: a time, a serial id, a key loaded in order), the scan visits superblocks in order of zone minimum of `x` and, before each, drops from the sets the values below the minimum of the superblocks still to come: no later row can hold them, so they are not counted twice, and the group's count already includes them. The answer is the plain scan's exact one. The sweep runs in one process and does not spill the group table (a spill would drop pairs from the sets it dumps): when that table would outgrow `hash_mem`, or a unit has rows the zones do not bound (a write buffer, read first), the scan restarts in plain order. `EXPLAIN ANALYZE`: `Distinct Stream Superblocks`, and `Distinct Stream Given Up` after a restart. A column in random order within every superblock, two sets, or a grouping set keeps the plain scan. `sift.agg_dist_stream = off` restores it.

What the pushed shapes do:

- **Text-valued leaves (`astext`) in paths and recordsets.** `jsonb_to_recordset(expr -> 'a') AS o(k text)` reads the leaf of a list of objects whose member is declared `astext`. A text literal tests it for equality (`=`, `IN`) only when no number or boolean prints as that text (it does not start with a digit or `-` and is not `true`/`false`), so the leaf text of the number 7 is never taken for the string `'7'`. A number or boolean literal against such a leaf, a strict path and a NOT of a test that may be NULL keep the normal plan (plain traps in `wl_path_parts_*`).
- **`EXISTS` over an index expression's array.** The planner deparses the expression with the query's own range table (the same expression in a sub-select's range table is what raised `invalid attnum`). One element's members are tested together, as for a column.
- **`x = ANY (ARRAY (SELECT ...))`.** An uncorrelated, stable sub-select that cannot raise is an InitPlan; the scan runs it once at start and its rows are the leaf's lookup table (as for a hashed sub-plan: no parallel workers). A sub-select that reads the row, is volatile or may raise keeps the normal plan (`wl_any_array_subselect_*`).
- **A sum of a numeric column times a value asked once.** `sum(amount * (SELECT count(*) FROM kinds))` and `sum(amount * $1)` (a generic plan) multiply each row by the value of a sub-select or a parameter, which the numeric programs of the scan (constants known when the plan is made) do not hold. The planner rewrites the aggregate to the product of the sum of the column and that value, made once for each group above the scan: numeric multiplication is exact, the display scale, NULL, NaN and the infinities come out the same. It applies to `sum` of a `numeric` product with a factor that reads no column (no correlated sub-select, nothing volatile), in the select list or the `HAVING`; an `avg`, a `DISTINCT` sum and a product of two columns stay as they were. The plan is taken where its cost is below half that of the plan as written.
- **A scan with a scalar sub-select that is a constant runs in workers.** The sub-select that gives one row whatever the data (an aggregate with no `GROUP BY`, a row by its whole unique key) is run by the leader when the scan starts, and the workers get its value with the spec: a condition `x > (SELECT avg(x) FROM t)` or an aggregate's argument over such a value is read by the workers as a constant. The rows of an `ARRAY` sub-select are the leader's lookup table and keep the scan in one process.
- **A sum of a numeric column times a value of a table joined by its unique key.** `sum(e.amount * k.rate) .. JOIN kinds k ON k.id = e.kind_id GROUP BY t.region` multiplies every row by the rate of its kind, which the numeric programs of the scan do per row. The kind of a row has one rate, so the rows of a kind and a group add up to `sum(e.amount)` times it: the planner groups the tables but the lookup by the keys of the query and the kind, adds up the amounts in the scan, joins the groups to the lookup and adds up the products (a few hundred groups where the rows are hundreds of thousands). Numeric multiplication is exact, so the display scale, NULL and NaN come out the same. It applies to inner joins, `sum` of a `numeric` product of a factor of the other tables and a value of the lookup alone, in the select list or the `HAVING`; a `sum` of a numeric column and `count(*)` or `count(x)` beside the products are added up in the sub-select as they are (the sum of the counts is cast back to the bigint, so the query has a `GROUP BY`: a count of no rows is 0, the sum of no counts NULL); an `avg`, `DISTINCT`, a `FILTER`, a float sum, a column of the lookup in a group or a condition, a column compared with a constant and a lookup that is not read by its whole unique key keep the plan as written. The plan is taken where its cost is below half that of the plan as written.
- **Fused arms over a text leaf.** The inner `sum`/`avg` of a float8 expression the scan evaluates (a cast, `CASE`) is accumulated per row like typed leaves. The expression runs for every item the scan reads, so the arms must share one WHERE (the scan's) and have no inner `FILTER`; an arm's own WHERE keeps the arms one by one. The plain cast raises for a non-numeric score, in the scan as in the heap plan.

### The comparison contract

Each result is materialized in a temporary table and compared by `coldiff.compare` (`test/col/run.sql`):

- columns (names, types, typmods) and row count are equal; a query needs a total `ORDER BY` and rows are compared by position. An analytics file has no `ORDER BY` but a `-- key:` line: both results are sorted by those columns, then by every column as text;
- every type but floats is equal as text: integers, dates, text, booleans, numerics (scale counts: `1.50` is not `1.5`), jsonb;
- `float4`/`float8`: `|x - y| <= 1e-12 + 1e-9 * max(|x|, |y|)` (`--abs`, `--rel`), because parallel workers and SIMD lanes add in another order. NaN equals NaN, an infinity only itself, `-0` equals `0`, NULL only NULL. This is no license for float4: `sum(float4)` adds in float4 in PostgreSQL, so a plan adding in double differs by about 1e-7 relative and fails (query `t_float4_sum`);
- a query that raises (a cast, integer or float overflow) must raise the **same SQLSTATE** in every variant (the message may name another row's value); such a file says `-- error: yes`, and a reference run that fails otherwise is a failure.

A per-order aggregate (`avg(x)`, `sum(x)` of a float under an outer aggregate) adds one order's elements from first to last in double and divides by their count: the value PostgreSQL gets when its plan feeds an order's rows in array order (a hash aggregate over the unnest). PostgreSQL's own plans disagree on last digits (a plan that sorts by order and key adds equal keys in its unstable sort order). A condition on the average (`avg >= 9`) that holds exactly for a decimal sum (8.7 + 9.0 + 9.3 over three) can then count a few more or fewer orders in one plan than another (three of 100 000 orders between the two arms of one query on the normal plan), whatever the scan does; the scan's count is the first-to-last one. A threshold that must be reproducible therefore uses integer values (exact sums) or leaves a margin, never the last digit of a float sum.

A differing result always fails (exit 1) and prints the first differing rows of both; nothing is silenced, and a query without a result (an unexpected error) fails too.

### The shape expectation

A file states the expected plan: `-- expect: agg` (the pushdown must happen once implemented), `plain` (it must not: volatile functions, unsupported or risky semantics) or `any`. Only `on` is checked for `agg`; every `on*` variant for `plain`. A mismatch is a `NOTE` in the coverage table's last column and in the summary line; it fails only with `--strict-shape`. The coverage table has, per query and set: shape, expectation, `equal` or `DIFF`, whether `on` used SiftAgg, reference and `on` times in ms, and the speedup.

### Corpus format

One query per file, as in `bench/sql/analytics/queries`: header lines, then the query. Header lines that are not a tag below are the title.

| tag | meaning |
|---|---|
| `-- shape: metrics-summary` | the shape of the query (the name of an analytics file of the form `metrics-summary` or `whole-top-category-kind` is its shape) |
| `-- expect: agg\ | plain\ | any` | the shape expectation (default `any`) |
| `-- sets: K10 K400 K4000` | the query runs once per set of `analytics_sets` (`{stores}`, `{sources}`, `{span(col)}`, `{range(col)}` are expanded as `bench/steps/analytics.py` does); `-` or absent: once |
| `-- key: 1 2` | sort both results by these columns first; `-` compares by position (the query has a total `ORDER BY`); absent: sort by every column |
| `-- scenario: yes` | part of the subset that the scenarios re-run |
| `-- guc: name = value` | a setting for every run of the file (repeatable) |
| `-- prepare: (int[], date)` and `-- args: ...` | the body is a prepared statement with `$1`...; the runs are `EXECUTE`s (with `plan_cache_mode` in a `guc` for a generic plan) |
| `-- requires: SELECT ...` | the file runs only if the statement returns true (repeatable), e.g. a collation that exists |
| `-- wrap: subquery` | materialize `SELECT * FROM (query)`, for a statement that `CREATE TABLE AS` does not take (`FOR UPDATE`) |
| `-- error: yes` | the query raises an error in the reference run |
| `-- error: either` | the query raises an error in the reference run and in the scan; their SQLSTATE may differ (a value the index cannot store: `0A000` against the cast error `22P02`) |
| `-- isolation: serializable` | the file runs in a transaction of that isolation level |

The sections `--@ pg`, `--@ flat`, `--@ ch` of an analytics file are cut: only `pg` runs. `-- tol:` is ignored (the contract above is the tolerance).

### Scenarios

After the fresh run, the fixture subset (`-- scenario: yes`) is compared again after DML that leaves the heap in every state the column index must answer for (`--scenarios`, default all). They are cumulative and only equality is checked:

| scenario | heap state |
|---|---|
| `insert` | 20% more rows (also of `t_jp` and `t_eo`) without a `VACUUM` (pages not all-visible) |
| `update` | an UPDATE of indexed columns (new tuple versions) and of the jsonb documents (also of `t_jp` and `t_eo`) |
| `delete` | hard deletes (also of `t_jp` and `t_eo`) and soft deletes (`deleted_at` set) |
| `abort` | a rolled-back INSERT, UPDATE and DELETE (dead tuples before any `VACUUM`) |
| `vacuum` | `VACUUM` after all that |
| `hot` | UPDATE of columns no index holds: HOT updates (the count is printed) |
| `recycle` | DELETE, `VACUUM (TRUNCATE off)`, then INSERT: new rows on the TIDs of deleted ones (the count is printed) |
| `held` | a second session with an uncommitted INSERT, UPDATE and DELETE next to the corpus, at READ COMMITTED and inside a REPEATABLE READ transaction; then a REPEATABLE READ snapshot taken before a writer commits |
| `maint` | `REINDEX`, then `sift.col_promote` and `sift.col_merge` when they exist |

### jsonpath truth tables

The fixtures `jp_*` read `t_jp`, whose members `s` (text), `n` (int4) and `b` (boolean) take every value of their leaves (`1` and `1.0`, the empty string, non-ASCII text, `2147483647`), a JSON null and a missing key. The table compares an element `e` with a literal `L` of the member's type; a scalar or null element and a missing key count as a missing member:

| member of `e` | `@.k == L` | `@.k != L` | `!(@.k == L)` | `!(@.k != L)` |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| a value equal to `L` | true | false | false | true |
| another value of the type | false | true | true | false |
| JSON null | false | true | true | false |
| missing | false | false | true | true |
| a value of another JSON type | unknown | unknown | unknown | unknown |

Against a `null` literal, `== null` is true for a JSON null only, `!= null` for every present non-null member, and under `!()` the other rows. Each form pushed down equals the heap in every variant and scenario; a form that is not provable (the last row, a number against a float leaf, strict `!()`, `jsonb_path_match`) is a `plain` fixture. A member the leaf cannot hold (an array or object in a number leaf, a number out of range) keeps the normal plan, as do older units or write buffers with uncertain kinds for tests that need complete kinds (guaranteed by column minor 6; REINDEX or VACUUM FULL restores the guarantee).

### An element against a column of its row

The fixtures `eo_*` read `t_eo`: members `q` (int4), `q8` (int8), `f` (float8) of its array against columns `r4`, `r8`, `rf` of the same row, with NULL columns, a NaN and a `-0`, a JSON null and a missing member, empty arrays and a NULL document. Element `j` of row `i` holds the value `r4` has in row `i + j - 1`, so every row has elements equal to its neighbours' columns, plus rows that must not match. An element is compared with its own row's column (`= <> < <= > >=`, either side first), NULL on either side is unknown, and the element's other tests (`AND`, `OR`, `IS NULL`, a literal) apply to that element. An expression of the column, a column of another type class (int4 member against a float8 column), a non-constant `LIMIT`, `OFFSET` and a second array keep the normal plan (`eo_trap_*`).

### Bench mode

`--bench [--runs N]` runs only the first pass (no scenarios) with the median of N runs (default 5, after a warm run) of reference and `on`, and prints go/no-go lines: K4000 queries that must push down (`expect: agg`, and the analytics corpus's S shapes) at least 20 times faster than the reference; K10 and K400 ones that push down not slower (0.95). A missed target is printed and fails the run only with `--strict-shape`. Times include the result's temporary table.

### Self-test

`scripts/coldiff.sh --selftest` runs a subset of the fixture corpus plain against plain (must pass), then with `--mutate KIND`, which perturbs every `on*` result as a pushdown bug would. These must fail: `int` (+1), `float` (relative 1e-6, beyond tolerance), `text`, `null`, `rows` (last row lost), `dup` (first row twice), `order` (rows 1 and 2 swapped, seen by a position-compared query), `type` (a wider column type), `cols` (an extra column). `float-tiny` (relative 1e-11, inside tolerance) must pass; a mutation applied to no result fails the self-test.

## Plans that depend on what ran before

Only the choice between a shared CTE and its inline plan, and between the flattened form of a statement and the form as written, is kept between statements (`verdict.c`, USAGE.md, "The choice for a shared CTE is kept per backend"); every other choice of the planner is made from the statement, its statistics and the settings alone. The choice is kept under the statement without its constants, the size class of each table, the settings that move a plan and the files of the column indexes, so a statement of other constants meets the verdict of the one that made it. It is taken only if the plan it makes reads about the rows the verdict was made for (within 1.4 times, `VERDICT_BAND`), else the verdict kept for that size is used or the statement is planned in full. A plan can therefore differ by history only for a statement whose two variants cost about the same at the constants of the two statements, and then only until the next plan in full: an entry is read seven times, the eighth read plans in full and replaces it, and each plan in full that confirms it doubles the reads to the next (16, 32, 64). The same holds for a verdict another backend made (`sift.verdict_cache_kb`). The rows are the same whichever variant is planned. `SET sift.agg_verdict_cache = off` turns it off for the session: every statement is planned in full and nothing is kept, so its plan does not depend on what ran before (`sift.verdict_cache_kb = 0` only stops the sharing between backends).

## Plan-choice gate

`scripts/plangate.sh` guards the choice between the normal plan and `Custom Scan (SiftAgg)`. It builds the tables of `test/plan/gen.sql` (events, dims, facts, a month spine, a partitioned copy; `--rows`, default 30000, and `--seed`; rows are hashes of their number and `ANALYZE` reads all of them, so statistics repeat) and runs each shape of `test/plan/shapes.sql` three ways: native (`sift.enable_agg` off), chosen (the planner's own choice) and scan (table scans off, so the pushdown runs where the query allows). Results must agree by the differential suite's contract (floats within 1e-12 + 1e-9 of the larger), the plan chosen must be the expected one, and the scan-over-native planner cost ratio must stay in the shape's band, so drift shows before it flips a choice. For each shape the table prints plan chosen, expected plan, ratio and band, native and scan times (median of `--runs`, default 3) and a verdict: `win`/`lose` when the scan is chosen and faster/slower by more than 1 ms and 20%, `miss` when it is not chosen but would win by as much, else `tie`. A wrong result, unexpected plan or out-of-band ratio fails; so does a `lose`, unless `--warn-time` (the full tier runs at 50000 rows with it: hosted runners are noisy). The quick tier runs without timings (`--runs 0`). Expectations hold from 20000 to 50000 rows; a shape whose plan changes inside that range expects `either`.

`--lane tuned` runs the same shapes with a tuned planner profile (`seq_page_cost` 100, `random_page_cost` 4, `max_parallel_workers_per_gather` 8, `hash_mem_multiplier` 2, `work_mem` 4GB, `jit` off). Results must agree and a lose still fails; the plan and cost band are the lane's own, in `test/plan/bands.tsv` (the tuned profile prices a sequential page at 100, which moves every ratio by a regime: the stock bands do not hold), so a shape taking another plan or a ratio leaving its band fails on PostgreSQL 18, as in the stock lane (a warning on another major; quick and full tiers run the lanes: `plan-gate-tuned`, `plan-gate-table` and `plan-gate-table-tuned`). A band `any` in a lane's rows marks a scan path that exists at some sizes only (`h02` in the tuned lane): nothing to hold, nothing to miss; `--update` rewrites it, so restore it by hand. `seq_page_cost` above `random_page_cost` is the setting that moves the picks: it prices a scan's heap check at the sequential price, which can refuse the scan where the normal plan is many times slower.

`--table` runs the same shapes on the tables as `sift_table` (`test/plan/table.sql` drops the `sift_col` indexes, rewrites every table and partition into the table access method and declares the typed `doc` paths): the results must agree as ever, the plan and cost band are the lane's own in `test/plan/bands.tsv` (`table`, and `tabletuned` with `--lane tuned`), so a plan or ratio that leaves them fails on PostgreSQL 18. The shapes that stay on the normal plan there are the partitioned ones (`h01`, `h03`, `h04`, `h05`: a partitioned `sift_table` has no pushdown yet) and the three month-spine lookups (`f01`, `f03`, `f04`: the table's lookup join loads its rows slowly).

To add a shape, append three lines to `test/plan/shapes.sql`: `-- name: x`, `-- expect: new` and the query (a total `ORDER BY` with a `LIMIT`); run `scripts/plangate.sh --update` and read the diff: it writes the plan chosen and a band of a factor of 2 around the measured ratio (refreshing every shape's bands; a plan stays unless `new`). In another lane the same flag rewrites that lane's rows of `test/plan/bands.tsv` (the plan the lane chooses, `either` stays, and the band around its ratio): do it when a cost change moved the ratios on purpose, and say in the commit which change it was. A band is a factor of 2 around the ratio at the size it is recorded at, 30000 rows (the default). The ratio does not hold across sizes, and the estimate is right about it: the scan costs its start and its units, the normal plan its rows, so the scan-over-native cost ratio falls as the table grows. Over 101 shapes the median is 0.134, 0.082 and 0.067 at 30000, 100000 and 250000 rows (the ratio of the measured times, 0.109, 0.061 and 0.051; the estimate over the time stays between 1 and 1.8 at each size for the classes but the point lookups, records `planner-pl20-units-stock*`), and the drift is the same on the builds before and after the one-unit conversion of the cost models: at 50000 rows 30 of 106 shapes were outside the 30000 bands before it and 40 after, 70 at 100000. So the bands are the size's own: another size takes the rows `LANE@ROWS` of `test/plan/bands.tsv` (`scripts/plangate.sh --rows 250000 --update`, once for each lane, records them; the four lanes at 250000 rows are there and the full CI tier runs them, half a minute each), and a size with none is checked for the results and the loses only. A shape whose ratio is wrong at a size, not only low, shows as a band that does not follow its time: the estimate over the time ratio of `in_expr_key` doubles from 30000 to 250000 rows while the time ratio stays, 0.217 to 0.429 against 0.131 to 0.124.

### Plan-estimate calibration

`scripts/plangate.sh --planning` holds the planning cost: the instructions to plan each of the shapes with the scan on and off, under `perf stat`, against the ceilings of `test/plan/planning.tsv`. Skipped without perf, a warning on a major other than 18.

`scripts/plangate.sh --calibrate` checks that the planner's estimates rank the competing plans of a shape the way they rank when run, in instructions and not in milliseconds or an absolute cost. The 30 shapes of `test/plan/calibrate.tsv` (point, range, order and limit, count, group, join, aggregate; at 30,000 rows, `--lane stock` or `tuned`, serial plans) are planned each way the planner can go with EXPLAIN only (`Custom Scan (SiftAgg)` with the other scans off, a sequential scan, a b-tree index scan, a bitmap scan, PostgreSQL without sift, the planner's own choice), plans of the same shape are one, and each distinct plan runs under `perf stat` (user instructions of the backend, three runs, the instructions of planning taken off). It prints the table and fails when the plan the planner chooses takes more than 20% and 1,000,000 instructions more than the best plan. It also lists the pairs of plans the estimates rank the wrong way round by the same margin; those with a scan are the cost model's (`sift` in the last column), the others are PostgreSQL's own. It takes 30 seconds a lane, needs `perf` (without it the run is skipped) and runs in the full tier (`plan-gate-calibrate`, `plan-gate-calibrate-tuned`). Parallel plans are not measured: `perf -p` counts the leader only, and at 30,000 rows the planner chooses no plan with workers for any of the 30 shapes in either lane (18 of them get workers only with `parallel_setup_cost = 0`, a plan no estimate chooses); the workers decision is held by `agg_workers_few_units` and measured in the options row of `sift.agg_par_min_rows`. A regression test that asserts a plan the planner chose on its costs keeps the gap between the two prices beside the assertion; `scripts/plan-margin.sh` prints it for a statement or for the plan helpers of a test (`--tests`), and a gap under 25% is a tie that the statistics or the runner can turn.

Today (PostgreSQL 18, 30,000 rows): no chosen plan loses in either lane. Mis-ranked pairs:

- no pair with a scan: the scan is never ranked the wrong way round by the margin. `join_two_lookups` is the closest: PostgreSQL's own plan is priced 539 and the planner takes the scan at 583 (`sift.explain_why` shows it against the 934 it prices PostgreSQL's plan at with the scan on the table), and the scan takes 17.4 million instructions against 55.9. The scan forced with the other scans off is priced 645, because those settings change what the scan costs; it is not the plan the planner chooses.
- stock lane, PostgreSQL's own estimates: `count_all` index-only scan 861 against sequential 1,739 and 18.5 million instructions against 10.3; `distinct_one_parent` bitmap 1,017 against index 1,699 and 4.8 against 2.2; `deleted_rows` sequential 1,668 against index 3,069 and bitmap 1,805, and 20.6 million against 4.9 and 5.8.

All twelve are chosen right now (`plangate --calibrate`: no `known` wrong in the family of 23 shapes of links and lookups). Two changes made it so, and both use what the planner knows:

- The fan of a join that multiplies the rows is priced from the rows the join adds beyond the fact table (the estimate of the join's rows against its larger input), the bound columns of the scan and the aggregate's argument. A joined row takes about 55 instructions with a `count(*)` grouped by a column of ten values, 260 with `sum` of a column of the lookup, and 1,300 where a column of high cardinality gets a slot of the hash table for each value; the price was one constant (120 operators, and 360 for the rows of a slot) fitted to the last. It is now `AGG_FAN_ROW` for the row, `AGG_FAN_KEY` for each bound column (`AGG_FAN_KEY_WIDE` when the column has more than a quarter of the joined rows as distinct values, from its statistics), `AGG_FAN_ARG` for an argument of the lookup and `AGG_FAN_FLAG` for the flag of an `EXISTS`; a scan that carries an expression slot keeps the price fitted before, as the statements that fitted it are in the gate (`dimexpr_*`).
- The normal plan of a join that multiplies the rows is priced for the rows it hashes and aggregates: its `AggPath` carries `join_agg_extra` (the joined rows beyond the larger input, at `AGG_JOIN_NS` nanoseconds of the model's own unit), as `hash_agg_extra` does for a hash aggregate. PostgreSQL prices the rows of a hash join but the aggregate over them at the group count, and a `LEFT JOIN` over all parents is the shape where the scan's whole-load price (59,796) alone is above the normal plan's (38,811) while the normal plan takes ten times the instructions. The extra counts the rows that joins of tables add: the rows of a function (100 for each call of `jsonb_each_text` where a document has three keys), a sub-select or a CTE are PostgreSQL's estimate and the scan does not charge them either (a `CROSS JOIN LATERAL jsonb_each_text(doc)` under a CTE that two scalar sub-selects read added 2.0 million phantom rows, 81,800 units, and turned the choice of the scans into the shared plan: test `agg_cte_scalar_consumers`).

The same cost on 16 shapes of tables of 1,000,000 rows with about 333 entries for each parent (the join, `EXISTS`, `NOT EXISTS` and `LEFT JOIN` of 20, 200, 3,000 and all parents; the large-table joins of the keyed-load cases): the join over all parents (scan 6,712 M instructions against 27,700 M for the normal plan, priced 70,240 against 385,241 before) and the `EXISTS` of 3,000 parents (449.5 against 866.9 M, priced 32,550 against 31,885 before) were chosen as the normal plan and are the scan now; 15 of the 16 choose the scan. The prices of the joins at 20 and 200 parents fell by the same step (a join of 200 parents 26,120 to 5,031, the plan taking 452 M against 2,022 M) and the choices there did not change. What stays wrong or unmodelled:

- The cache misses of the lookup into the entries (three for each fact row at 1,000,000 rows, 96 instructions for each joined row) are not in the price; the difference to the measured scan is under the margin of the choices above.
- In the joins at 200 parents the best plan is the nested loop of index scans (61.6 million instructions, priced 30,402 against 24,862 for the hash join PostgreSQL chooses at 785): a wrong choice among PostgreSQL's own plans that no price of the scan changes.
- The warm planning of a `NOT EXISTS` that the scan keeps as the plain form costs what the native planner costs: PostgreSQL's `eqjoinsel` compares the most common values of both join columns, so it grows with `default_statistics_target` (the plan gate analyzes at 1000: `linkanti_all_parents` plans in 31 million instructions, and in 1.75 million at the default 100 against 1.37 million for the probe form). The probe rewrite (a lateral `LIMIT 1` child, `sift_probe`) is planned instead only where it is cheaper by price, and it has no such join to estimate. At 30,000 rows and 1,000 values, with the lookup cache off, one statement takes (planning + execution): probe form 0.5 + 6.9 ms, plain form 3.8 to 4.7 + 4.5 ms, native 4 + 10 to 14 ms; at the default target the plain form's planning is 0.4 million instructions above the probe form's and it wins by 2.4 ms of execution. The ceilings of `linkanti_*` in `test/plan/planning.tsv` are those of the plain form.
- At 20 parents the two plans take the same instructions (20.0 to 38.8 million against 22.6 to 36.4) and the time is the planning of the scan, 1 to 2 ms, which the 20% and 1 ms limit of the gate reads as a loss on some runs: those shapes are measured by the benchmark cases of the keyed load, not held here.
