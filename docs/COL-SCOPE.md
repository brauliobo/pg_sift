# Scope index

A scope is a key a `sift_col` index reads its rows by that the rows do not hold: they reach it through a table of `(member, scope key)` pairs. Events of entities, entities in tenants: the question
is "the events of tenant 7", and the tenant is in the pairs of the entity, not in the event. The index keeps one copy of the rows, its entity segments, and every entity segment of 16 units or more of an index with a time column gets a **scope sidecar**: for each scope key, the runs of units that hold the rows of its members, each with a summary of the key's rows in them (a cube of the run by calendar month). A read of one tenant takes the summaries of the runs whose pairs did not change since the sidecar was made, reads the other units by reference, and runs the join when that costs less. Opt-in. The option is new in 5.0, which stored the rows once for each scope key in scope segments; 5.1 replaces them with the sidecar (column format minor 53), and an index built by 5.0 is read through the join until `REINDEX` ([upgrading](UPGRADING.md#what-51-changes-for-a-scope-index)). `sift_table` has no scope. Unless a figure says otherwise, the figures on this page are user instructions of one backend, warm, on generated lab fixtures of 100,000 to 2,000,000 rows (blocks hit and read where stated): they say how the arms compare on those shapes, not what a real database will measure.

This page is about the index, in this order: [the expected case](#the-expected-case), [the declaration](#the-declaration), [what a read does](#what-a-read-does) and [what the planner recognises](#what-the-planner-recognises), [EXPLAIN](#explain) and the refusals, [what it costs](#what-it-costs), [the edges](#edges), what happens [without a scope index](#without-a-scope-index-reuse), [a denormalised table](#a-denormalised-table-not-shipped), and [the prior art](#prior-art-what-exists-and-what-differs).

## The expected case

A table of facts that many tenants (or groups) share, most of them small or medium, and requests that aggregate over one tenant or a few: sums and counts by kind or by month, a filter on a measure, distinct members, the top or the latest rows. Without a scope index such a read follows the table: it reads the units that hold a member of the tenant, so its cost grows with the rows of the table. With one it takes the summaries of the tenant's runs and reads the rest by reference.

Measured through the executor on a lab fixture (300,000 events of 80 entities in units of 3,700 rows, 51 scope keys, 626,630 entries, 2.09 entries a row), the sum and count by kind of one key, all time and one month, read with the units of the key's members by reference, with the runs of the sidecar, and (the last column) with the scope segments of 5.0 on the same data (instructions of one warm statement):

| statement | key | by reference | with the runs | 5.0 scope segments |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| sum and count by kind | 1 (75 members, 81 units) | 15.1 M | 2.45 M | 1.93 M |
| | 2 (13 members, 13 units) | 3.39 M | 1.72 M | 1.62 M |
| | 3 (16 units) | 3.96 M | 1.86 M | 1.76 M |
| | 14 (2 units) | 1.87 M | 1.60 M | 1.56 M |
| one month | 1 | 9.85 M | 2.86 M | 2.82 M |
| | 2 | 3.29 M | 2.14 M | 2.18 M |
| | 3 | 3.73 M | 2.28 M | 2.29 M |
| | 14 | 2.19 M | 2.00 M | 2.03 M |

The runs read 1.1 to 6.2 times fewer instructions than the units of the members, and 1.03 to 1.27 times the instructions of the 5.0 scope segments for the sum by kind of a big group, 0.98 to 1.01 times for one month, with the same rows. The price is the sidecar's size, its build and its upkeep ([What it costs](#what-it-costs)): on that fixture it adds 9.8% to the entity segments where the 5.0 scope segments added 118%. A filter on a column, a point lookup of one member, distinct members by month and a range that cuts a calendar month read the units of the key by reference; [the edges](#edges) say where the gain is small and what is not supported.

## The declaration

The recipe is one statement: the `scope` option on the index of the facts. `CREATE INDEX` makes the journal index of the table of pairs (it records the pairs that change) when the table has none, and says in a `NOTICE` which one it made, which btree of the pairs it recorded. The queries stay as the application writes them: the planner reads the join forms against the units (below).

    CREATE INDEX events_col ON events USING sift_col (id, dim_a_id, created_at, kind, value_num)
      WITH (scope = 'tenant_id: dim_a_id -> tenant_scope(dim_a_id, tenant_id)');

`key_name: fk -> [schema.]relation(member, scope_key)`: the name of the derived key, the column of the index that holds the member key (`int2`, `int4`, `int8`, `text`, `varchar` or `uuid`), and the relation of pairs with its
two columns: the member column, of the same kind as the member key of the index ([member types](#member-types)), and the scope key column, an integer, `text`, `varchar` or `uuid` ([key types](#key-types)). Up to 4 scopes, separated by `;`, each with its own name. The relation is a heap table (not a view, not the indexed table), with an immediate btree on exactly `(member,
scope_key)` in either order, unique (a primary key or a unique constraint) or not ([a pair may be several rows](#pairs-in-several-rows-a-multiset)), both columns NOT NULL; an unqualified name is in the schema of the indexed table. The index depends on that btree: `DROP INDEX`
of it, or `DROP TABLE` of the relation, needs `CASCADE`, which drops the index. The dependency is also the record of which btree defines the pairs: `CREATE INDEX`
chooses it (a unique one before a non-unique one, then the lowest oid of those that qualify) and says which in a NOTICE, every read uses that one, and a btree made or rebuilt (`REINDEX .. CONCURRENTLY`) afterwards changes nothing:
`REINDEX` of the scope index (also `REINDEX .. CONCURRENTLY`) and `ALTER INDEX` keep the recorded btree, so what the index means cannot change under a rebuild; a new `CREATE INDEX` chooses again. `ALTER INDEX .. SET (scope = ..)`, `SET (scope = 'off')` and `RESET (scope)` are refused once the index holds a segment (`0A000`, `cannot change the column definitions of index ".." while it holds data`): the scope is part of how the segments were written, so changing a scope or giving it up means building the index again beside the old one, `CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY` with the declaration or without it and then `DROP INDEX` the old one (no scan is lost while it is built). An index with no segment takes the change.
`cols(..)` and `max_multiplicity(n)` after the relation of a 5.0 declaration (`tenant_id: dim_a_id -> tenant_scope(dim_a_id, tenant_id) max_multiplicity(5)`) are accepted and ignored, with a `NOTICE` at `CREATE INDEX` and `REINDEX`: the index keeps one copy of the rows, so no column is left out of it and no number of keys a member has is too many. An index that stores them in its option keeps working, and a dump that names them restores. `sift.scope_max_multiplicity` and the scope segments of 5.0 are gone ([upgrading](UPGRADING.md#what-51-changes-for-a-scope-index)).
The sidecar summarises its runs by the calendar months (UTC) of the first `date`, `timestamp` or `timestamptz` column of the index; an index without one has no sidecar and its scope reads run the join.
A scope may also be the union of this relation and a relation joined through a second one ([two paths](#pairs-reached-by-two-paths-a-direct-grant-and-a-group-grant)).

**Privileges.** The build reads every pair of the relations of the scope (the sidecar summarises the rows of every key's members), so the role that runs `CREATE INDEX` or `REINDEX` needs what a
reader of the join needs: `SELECT` on the relation, or on its member and scope key columns and on the columns that the predicate of the btree of the pairs reads, and no row-level security of the relation that applies to it (the sidecar
would summarise the rows the policies hide from it); otherwise the statement is refused with `permission denied`, naming the column and the `GRANT` (or the role without the policies). `ALTER INDEX` reads nothing and checks nothing: the `REINDEX` that writes
the segments does. A statement that reads the scope is checked as the join as written is, because PostgreSQL keeps the relation of the pairs in the range table of the statement: a role without `SELECT` on it, one whose `SELECT` was revoked after
the build and one that has it on a column the statement does not use but not on one it does gets `permission denied` from the scope read and from the join alike, and a role the policies of the relation apply to reads the join as written
(`sift.explain_why`, arm `scope`). The test is `col_scope_privileges`.

**The journal index.** The relation of pairs needs a `sift_col` index with `track_changes = 'ctid'` whose first column is the member, that holds the scope key and that has no `WHERE` (a partial index never hears of a pair inserted
outside its predicate, so it does not qualify). `CREATE INDEX` uses the first one the relation has (a scope of two paths needs one for each of its three relations). When there is none, a plain `CREATE INDEX` makes it, as the statement
you would write, named by the rule of PostgreSQL for an index (`tenant_scope_dim_a_id_tenant_id_sift_journal`), owned by the owner of the table of pairs (a role that does not own it is refused, with the statement for the owner in the hint), and says so in a `NOTICE`. The
index carries a comment that a dump keeps, and goes with the last scope index that depends on it: `DROP INDEX`, `DROP TABLE` or `ALTER TABLE .. DROP COLUMN` of that scope index drops it in the same transaction, unless
another scope index still uses it. A journal made by hand, or one whose comment you change, is yours and stays. The writers of the table of pairs wait until the statement ends, as for any `CREATE INDEX` on it (the statement takes
`SHARE ROW EXCLUSIVE` on the table first, so that two builds that make or use its journal do not deadlock); to name the journal, put it in a tablespace, build it without blocking the writers or keep it, make it first:

    CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY tenant_pairs_journal ON tenant_scope USING sift_col (dim_a_id, tenant_id) WITH (track_changes = 'ctid');

A `CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY` of the scope index holds nothing stronger than `ShareUpdateExclusiveLock` on its table while it reads the table and writes its segments, as PostgreSQL's own concurrent builds do, and the writers of the table go on. The first scope index
declared on a relation starts its journal before its build reads anything and stamps its sidecars with it, and the writers of the relation of pairs wait for the statement, as those of the table do for a `CREATE INDEX`. A concurrent build starts the journal at the end of its build instead, in a window of a few milliseconds in which it takes `ShareLock` on the relation of pairs (after waiting for the writers that hold it, without queuing the ones that come after) and writes the sidecars in its validation pass, under `ShareUpdateExclusiveLock` only, under the stamp
of the journal, as the maintenance does (the entity segments are written first, and no writer waits for the second pass), so a scope read takes the runs as soon as the pass ends. When the relation of pairs has no journal, the concurrent build makes it at that point as a journal that reads no row (its `NOTICE` says so), in the same few milliseconds: the writers of the relation of pairs wait only until that transaction ends, as for the triggers of any concurrent build. The journal is then ready for writes and not valid, and the validation pass fills it in the phases PostgreSQL gives a concurrent index: it waits for the writers that began before the journal was visible (nothing is held and nothing queues behind it), reads the relation of pairs under a snapshot taken after them and adds the pairs the journal does not have (`validate_index`; they predate the journal, so no journal record is written for them, and a read whose snapshot does not see the transaction that made the journal as committed does not read the log and takes the heap: that check is what makes the missing records safe), waits for the transactions whose snapshots are older, and makes the journal valid, holding `ShareUpdateExclusiveLock` only. Only the statement that made it reads it as a journal meanwhile; a statement that fails or is cancelled leaves an invalid index that `DROP INDEX` removes, with the scope index it was made for, and no other scope index takes it. Building the journal first with `CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY` as above names it and keeps it, and the scope index then finds it. A restore brings the journal back as the index it is, before the scope index that reads it.

**`ALTER INDEX` and `REINDEX` without `CONCURRENTLY`.** `ALTER INDEX .. SET (scope = ..)`, `SET (track_changes = ..)` and `RESET (scope)` are DDL that is not concurrent, like `CREATE INDEX` without `CONCURRENTLY` in PostgreSQL: they take `ShareLock` on the table (and the statement that makes a missing journal, `ShareRowExclusiveLock` on the relation of pairs) until the statement ends, the sidecar is written under it, and the writers of those relations wait for a time that grows with the size of the table. Use `CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY` or `REINDEX INDEX CONCURRENTLY` when they must not. A restore brings the journal back as the index it is, before the scope index that reads it.

## What a read does

A scan of the index with a scope conjunct (the rows whose member has a pair with a key) reads the entity rows of the members the scope relation lists for the keys, under the snapshot of the statement: the member keys come from the btree of the pairs (the leaves of its journal index, or the relation) and the scan tests the rows against them as a join key filter on the member column. Where an entity segment has a sidecar, the scan takes the **runs** of the key in the place of the units they stand for. A run is taken when

- no member whose pair changed since the stamp of the sidecar lies in the span of the run (the smallest and largest member key of its units; for a text, varchar or uuid member, which has no order, the member words its Bloom filter holds; the journal says which members changed), and
- every unit of the run is kept by the zones and the comparisons of the scan, is clean (no delete or update has touched it) and lies on all-visible pages.

A taken run answers from its summary, without reading a row: the histograms of the dims its cube holds (grouped aggregates over all time or over whole calendar months, UTC), and the ungrouped `count`, `sum`, `min` and `max` of an integer measure or dim of the cube (derived from the cubes, no bytes of their own). A statement whose time test cuts a calendar month, a month that lacks the dim it groups by, a comparison on a column, a point lookup of one member, `rollup_columns`, id sets (`count(DISTINCT member)` by month), a time zone other than UTC and the aggregates of a leaf the cube lacks are not answered from a run: the run is split back into its units, which are read by reference. Rows of segments with no sidecar, of units no run covers and of the write buffers are entity rows, read by reference.

The members come from the pairs under the snapshot, so a pair added or removed since the sidecar was made is in the list at once. A member added has no run; a member removed is not listed; a run whose span holds a member whose pair changed is read by its units, and the runs of the other members, and of the other keys, keep standing for theirs. The rows are the join's in every state the tests move the index through (`agg_scope_side`: a fresh sidecar, then pair edits, then row edits, with and without the runs; `col_side_build`). The scan takes the scope conjunct from the join the way an application writes it (the recognition below).

## What the planner recognises

A statement that joins the scanned table to the scope relation of one of its scope indexes is planned twice: as written, and with that join taken out of the `FROM` list and its two
conditions, the equality of the member column with the declared column of the table and the binding of the scope key, made the scope conjunct of the scan (`EXPLAIN`: `Scope Index`). The
estimated costs of the two plans choose (and, at execution, the real keys: below): the scope read leaves the share of the rows that the pairs of the keys own (`pg_stats` of the key column of the relation, the most common keys by their
own frequency) of the distinct members of the table, where the join's key filter on the member column leaves the units that hold a member of the keys, most of them for facts ordered by time. The
rows are those of the join in every case; whether the index may be read at execution is the reader's verdict (`Scope Fallback`).

| form | counts a row | recognised |
|---|---|---|
| `JOIN scope_rel s ON s.member = f.fk WHERE s.scope_key = k` (also `IN (k1, k2)` with one distinct key) | once for each pair | yes, where the recorded btree is unique; with a non-unique one (a [multiset](#pairs-in-several-rows-a-multiset)) a join counts a fact once for each row: no |
| `f.fk IN (SELECT member FROM scope_rel WHERE scope_key = k or IN (list))`, `EXISTS`, `f.fk = ANY (ARRAY(SELECT member ..))` (planned also as the `IN`), a CTE of the members, a set-returning SQL function of them | once | yes, any number of constant keys |
| the same through a view, a security barrier view that projects the two columns, an inlined `STABLE` SQL function | as written | yes |
| a scope of two paths: `f.fk IN (SELECT member FROM rel WHERE key = k AND <predicate> UNION [ALL] SELECT m.member FROM rel2 g JOIN vrel m ON m.via = g.via WHERE g.key = k AND <predicates>)`, `EXISTS`, the same through a view or an inlined function | once | yes, any number of constant or parameter keys, the same in both selects |
| a scope of two paths: `JOIN (SELECT .. UNION SELECT ..) v ON v.member = f.fk WHERE v.key = k` (the `UNION` distinct, the member and the key projected, or the member alone), also through a view | once for each pair (once with the member alone) | yes, for one key, several keys, every key and the key as a group key: the node holds the join over the union, whose plan is a child of the node, and runs it; `UNION ALL` under a join counts a member that both paths reach twice and keeps the join |
| `JOIN` with several keys (`= ANY ($1)`, `IN (k1, k2)`, a table of keys) | once for each pair | yes, where the plan holds the join as written (below): the node runs it, which counts a row for each pair |
| `JOIN scope_rel s ON s.member = f.fk GROUP BY s.scope_key` (every key: with other group columns, aggregates, `HAVING`, `ORDER BY` the key, a top-N over the groups, `count(DISTINCT ..)`; the key of type `int2`, `int4` or `int8` is the group column's type) | once for each pair | yes, and the node runs the join as written (the rows hold no value of the key to group by); `WHERE s.scope_key = ANY (..)` with the key grouped by is the same read of those keys |
| the key in an expression, an aggregate of it, `ROLLUP` / `CUBE` / grouping sets or `DISTINCT ON` over it, another column of the relation read, a range of keys, `GROUP BY` the key with a `LEFT JOIN` | | no |
| the scope key bound by a parameter: `= $1`, `= ANY ($1)`, `IN ($1, $2)`, a stable expression (`current_setting(..)::int`), the output of an InitPlan (`= (SELECT ..)`, `= ANY (ARRAY(SELECT ..))`) | as written | yes: the keys are those of the execution (below); a join (a row for each pair) only for a scalar, an array may hold several keys |
| a table of keys: `s.key IN (SELECT key FROM keys WHERE ..)`, `EXISTS`, `JOIN keys k ON k.key = s.key` | as written | yes, read once when the scan starts (below); a join (a row for each pair) only where the table gives one key: a unique index on the key column with the other columns bound, or a `DISTINCT` sub-select |
| the conditions of the relation that are the predicate of its partial unique btree (`AND s.deleted_at IS NULL`, in the `WHERE`, an `ON`, a plain view or a sub-select), stated as the btree has it | as written | yes: the pairs are the rows that satisfy it |
| a statement that does not state the predicate of the btree (the soft deleted rows are rows of the join and no pairs of the units), another condition on the relation, a join to a relation of a scope of two paths that is not the union of its two paths (its pairs are the union of the direct grants and those of the groups, the rows of none of them), a union with one select, another key or no key in a select, a select without the predicate of its btree or with another condition, `DISTINCT`, `LIMIT` or an aggregate in a select, a view with a condition of its own (`security_barrier`), `DISTINCT` or a join inside the sub-select, a view over a relation that has child tables or a sample (their rows are rows of the join and no pairs), `NOT EXISTS`, an outer join, row-level security on the relation, the relation inside a join in the sub-select of a semi join (`IN (SELECT .. WHERE x IN (SELECT .. FROM scope_rel ..))`) or in a sub-select that is no select of it alone (lateral, of several tables), in a plan of its own (`NOT IN`, `OR`, `f.fk = ANY (ARRAY(SELECT member FROM scope_rel WHERE ..))`, an InitPlan that runs before the scan and is no join to recognise: it is read as `IN (SELECT ..)` (the planner hook plans both and keeps the cheaper plan) where the sub-select has no `ORDER BY`, `LIMIT`, `DISTINCT` or aggregate and the member is a column of the scanned table; a scalar sub-select, a `MATERIALIZED` CTE or one used twice, the select list, an aggregate argument, `HAVING`), under a partitioned scan, a second join to a scope relation next to the one that is read | | no, and `sift.explain_why` says which |

**The predicate of the pairs.** The pairs are the rows of the unique btree of the relation that satisfy its predicate, so a statement is read by the scope only when the rows it takes from the relation, for the key it
binds, are those: every other condition on the relation must be implied by the predicate (it filters no pair out) and together they must imply it (it takes no row that is not a pair). `WHERE s.tenant_id = 4` over a btree
`WHERE deleted_at IS NULL` counts the soft deleted rows too, which are no pairs, so the join runs as written (`the pairs of the scope are the rows of its unique btree that satisfy (deleted_at IS NULL): the statement does not state
it`); `WHERE s.tenant_id = 4 AND s.deleted_at IS NULL` is the pairs and is read by the scope. A plain btree has no predicate and the relation no other condition. A condition that reads a parameter is proved only by a custom plan
(its value is a constant there): a generic plan keeps the join.

**Parameters.** A key that is a parameter of a prepared statement (or the output of an InitPlan, or a stable expression) is read when the scan starts, so a generic plan has the scope read for every value: the plan
is made once, and each execution has the keys, the pairs and the valid runs of its own snapshot (a cached plan stays exact when the pairs change between two executions; NULL and the NULLs of an
array match nothing, an empty set reads no unit). `EXPLAIN` shows `Scope Keys: $1`, and a run `Scope Keys: $1 (2 keys)`. A custom plan has the constants and is priced for them. A generic plan has no value to price: a scalar is the pairs of the average key
(the rows `pg_stats` expect for an equality on the key column), an array that is not a list the 10 keys PostgreSQL assumes for an array it does not know, and the choice between the scope read and the join is made at execution, for the keys the
execution has (below).

**A table of keys.** The keys of a user, a team or a role are a table, and the statement reads them in a join (`JOIN keys k ON k.key = s.key WHERE k.user_id = 7`), an `IN` or an `EXISTS`. The planner takes the table out of
the join and has the scan read its keys once when it starts, in the statement's snapshot and with the table's own conditions (as the output of an InitPlan: `Scope Keys: ARRAY(SELECT ..)`), so the keys of each execution of a
prepared statement are the rows of that moment. The table is read by the scan's own sub-query and no longer by the statement, so it must be a plain heap or
partitioned table the role reads without row-level security, that the statement does not modify, with conditions that are immutable or stable and cannot raise an error. A join counts a fact once for each pair and each
row of the key table, so it is recognised only where the table holds the key once (a primary or unique non-partial index on the key column, the other columns bound by `=`, or a `DISTINCT` / `GROUP BY` sub-select);
`IN` and `EXISTS` count once and take any number of keys. A key table that is a view, a materialized or foreign table, a sub-select, a table with row-level security, one that is joined to another table or whose columns the
statement reads, keeps the join and says why in `sift.explain_why`. The price is made of the key table's rows (the estimate of its conditions; 10 for an unbounded set) and the pairs of the average key, so
a user of the few largest tenants of a skewed set is priced for the average ones, and the node decides again for the keys the execution reads (below).

**The scope read or the join, for the keys of the execution.** The plan of a scope read holds the join as written too (the first pass's scan, packed with the node), with its price in instructions at 1, 3, 10, 30 and 100 keys of the average size and at every key (linear between them: `AGG_ALT_ROW` a row of the table, `AGG_ALT_PAIR` a pair of the relation, `AGG_ALT_OP` an operator of the model beyond the first key's, `src/agg/cost/model.h`). When the scan starts it counts what the sidecars hold of the key (its runs, their units and rows, the rows of the segments that have a sidecar, and the units and rows of those that have none) and prices the read with the terms of `src/col/scope.h`: the set-up, a unit of pre-aggregates for each run, a unit read by reference for each other unit, the aggregation of each row of those units, and each pair of the relation the member list reads. It runs the join where that costs less: `Scope Fallback: join is cheaper for 30 keys` (`for every key` for a read of every key). A key that has no run in any segment (no sidecar, a key whose members fill one unit, a key the value store of a text, varchar or uuid key lacks, a key after those a full scope kept) runs the join, and `EXPLAIN` says `Scope Fallback: no sidecar holds a run of the key`. A rescan decides again; `EXPLAIN` always says which way the node went. The join runs with the workers of the plan, as the scan it replaces would (see **Workers**). The generic plan has the same alternative: the key count it was priced for is the 10 PostgreSQL assumes for an array, the execution has the real one. No plan holds the alternative where the join as written has no plan of this scan (the keys are the output of an InitPlan array, or the join is to a plan of its own: a table of keys read through a join that is not a semi join of one key), where the scope index is on a view, or where the join as written has no plan with as few workers as the scan has: those keep the choice of the plan (the estimated costs). A statement that is nearly one keeps the join and says why in `sift.explain_why` (arm `scope`, slug `scope`). `SET sift.agg_scope = off` turns the recognition off; a top-N, a `GROUP BY` with an `ORDER BY .. LIMIT` and the aggregates are the scan's as ever.

**Reads that always run the join.** A scan of the entity rows by the member list counts a fact once, and the rows hold no value of the scope key to group by. So the node runs the join, without a price, for a group by the scope key (`a group by the scope key`) and several keys that count a row for each pair (`a row for each pair of several keys`: a join, or `GROUP BY` the key). A scope that has no run (an integer member that is not the order of the units, an index with no time column, a segment of fewer than 16 units) runs the join or reads by reference. A read of these shapes in a node that holds no join (the planner kept the scope conjunct without the alternative) is an error that says what to do (`a scope read of .. needs the join`, hint: rewrite the statement over the join to the scope relation, or `SET sift.agg_scope` to off).

**Many to many.** A member may be in several keys. A read of one key counts each fact of a member that has the pair once. A read of several keys with the meaning of `IN` or `EXISTS` counts a fact once whichever of the keys its member has (`Scope Semantics: once`): the scan tests the fact's member against the members listed for all the keys, so a member of two keys is listed once. A join and `GROUP BY` the key count a fact once for each pair (`Scope Semantics: per pair`): one key counts each pair once, and several keys or every key run the join.

**Changed pairs.** The runs are what a change of the pairs can make wrong, and the journal says which runs: a run is taken only when no member whose pair changed since the stamp of the sidecar lies in its span, so a change costs the runs it lies in, not the key's. Those runs are read by reference, tested against the member list, until the maintenance makes the sidecar again (`sift.col_scope_refresh_pct`, [What it costs](#what-it-costs)). When many members changed, the units that are read by reference cost the instructions of reading them, which the node prices against the join as above. Test `agg_scope_side`: after a pair edit the key whose members changed reads its units, and the keys whose members did not change keep their runs. Test `agg_scope_side_take`: a run is taken whichever item of the scan holds its units (the items of a scan end where no run of its key is cut) and whichever of its units a time range leaves, at the first of them the cursor reaches; the runs over an item boundary and those of a range late in the history are all taken, with the rows of the join. A merge makes the sidecar from the pairs as committed, not those of the transaction that calls it (it may roll back; the journal counts its changes as changes since the stamp once it commits).

**Every key, grouped by the key.** A read of every key (`Scope Keys: all`) or grouped by the scope key (`Scope Group Key: <key_name>`) counts a row for each pair, and the entity rows hold no value of the key. The planner recognises the join (`JOIN scope_rel s ON s.member = f.fk GROUP BY s.key`; tests `agg_scope_group_key`, `col_scope_groupkey`), plans it as written and again with the relation taken out, and the node runs the join as written. The 5.0 scope segments read every key from the rows stored for it.

## EXPLAIN

A statement that the scope read cannot answer is still answered, by the join as written, and says why. `EXPLAIN` shows `Scope Index` when the scan carries the scope and `Scope Fallback: <why>` when it did not read the entity rows of the members (the planner kept the conjunct as a filter, or the node ran the join); `SELECT * FROM sift.explain_why('<query>')` (arm `scope`) returns the reason without running the statement. `SET sift.agg_scope = off` gives the join as written for a comparison.

`Scope Index`, `Scope Keys` (`all` for every key), `Scope Group Key` (a group key of the scan is the derived key) and `Scope Semantics`; in a run `Scope Fallback: <why>`, `Scope Members Listed` (the member keys the pairs list for the keys), `Scope Sidecar Runs: N` (the runs of the sidecar the scan took in the place of their units), `Scope Units` (read and skipped) and `Scope Rows Read` (0 when the runs answered all of them). The reasons (the labels are in [COL-PLAN](COL-PLAN.md)): those of the planner (`disabled`, `no scope of that name is declared on the member key`, `pairs are a multiset (non-unique btree): a join counts a fact once for each row`, and the others listed there) and those of the node: `a group by the scope key`, `a row for each pair of several keys`, `join is cheaper for N keys` (the price of the runs and the units by reference is over the join's: the node ran the join as written), `no sidecar holds a run of the key` (a read of one key that no sidecar of the index has a run for: a segment of fewer than 16 units has no sidecar, a key whose members fill one unit of a segment has no run in it, and the keys after the first of a scope whose run records passed their share are cut, [What it costs](#what-it-costs); the join runs without a price) and `scope segments of an older format (REINDEX)` (an index built by 5.0, whose scope segments no read uses). A scope read is decided once, by the leader (the keys, the members listed and whether the join runs are made from the snapshot of the statement); the workers of a `Gather` read that decision, not their own (see **Workers**).

**Where the conjunct may be.** The scan takes the conjunct when it filters the rows the scan reads: the `WHERE` of the statement (when no outer join of the scan can null the scanned table), the conditions
inside the nullable input of an outer join (the `ON` conjuncts that read only that input, the `WHERE` of a sub-select or of an inlined CTE: pushing them below the join is exact), a lateral child, a
materialized CTE (its own scan). The dashboard shape, a small table of periods left-joined to the scoped events, takes the runs of the key. A conjunct that does not filter the scanned rows
stays an ordinary filter, which the scan tests on every row, and `EXPLAIN` says why in `Scope Fallback`: the `ON` clause of a join that keeps the scanned rows (a `LEFT JOIN` from the events, a `FULL
JOIN`: the rows that fail it are output rows), a conjunct over the rows an outer join made of the unmatched ones (`WHERE conj OR e.id IS NULL` on the nullable side: the rows the join made pass
it), an expression around it, a key that is not a constant, a second scope (a scan reads one), the setting off. A `WHERE conj` over the nullable side of a `LEFT JOIN` drops the unmatched rows, which
the scan cannot do: the statement keeps the normal plan (`sift.explain_refusals`: `outer_join`).

## What it costs

The index keeps its entity segments as the same index without a scope would write them, and adds the sidecar to each of 16 units or more. `CREATE INDEX` and `REINDEX` write the sidecars after the entity segments; a concurrent build writes them in its second pass, under the stamp of the journal, with the writers of the table going on. The merge step of the maintenance (and `VACUUM`) writes the sidecar of an entity segment that has none, and writes it again, from the pairs of the scope relation read once, when more than `sift.col_scope_refresh_pct` percent of the rows of its runs sit in a run with a member whose pair changed since its stamp or when the journal the stamp names is gone. The sidecar is a part of its segment (whole pages after the segment's own stream, [FORMATS.md](FORMATS.md)): a merge or a rewrite writes a segment without one and drops the old with the old segment, so no sidecar names units that are gone. A segment of fewer than 16 units has none; its rows are read by reference.

**Every scope of the index has its record in the sidecar of a segment** (up to 4, each with the stamps of its own journals), made together from the pairs of each scope read once under one snapshot; the sidecar of a segment is made again for all its scopes when one of them has no record or has runs the journals no longer vouch for. A scope whose journals do not vouch when the sidecar is made has no record, and its reads run the join until the next `REINDEX` or merge step. The journals refuse the snapshot of a `SERIALIZABLE` transaction and of one that has run a write the index has not heard yet (the first transaction of a backend that loaded the library in the middle of a statement that writes): a `sift.merge` there writes its segment without the sidecar of the one it replaces, and the next merge step writes it (test `col_scope_side_merge_open`). The units of a merge are sized by the session that merges (`sift.col_unit_rows`; the maintenance worker sizes them from the statistics), so a merge that writes coarser units than the build did can leave a segment under 16 units, which has none. A read of a key takes the runs of its own scope: an edit of the pairs of one scope names no member of the others and leaves their runs as they were (test `col_side_scopes`).

**Size and build.** On the lab fixture (300,000 rows, 80 entities, units of 3,700 rows, 51 scope keys, 626,630 entries for the rows, 2 entity segments; 538,591 of the entries are in 32 runs of 172 units, the others belong to keys whose members fill one unit and are read by reference; one backend, instructions of the backend; the bytes are what `CREATE INDEX` adds to the file, the median of three runs for the instructions):

| | 5.0 scope segments | 5.1 sidecar, `maintenance_work_mem` 64 MB | 5.1 sidecar, 8 MB |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| bytes added to the 4,177,920 of the entity segments | 4,931,584 B (+118%) | 360,448 B (+8.6%; 7.3% of the 5.0 bytes) | 393,216 B (+9.4%; 8.0% of the 5.0 bytes) |
| instructions of the scope part of the build | 5.26 G, 8.4k an entry | 0.55 G, 870 an entry | 0.54 G, 858 an entry |
| temporary bytes of the build | 14.1 MB | 0 | 0 |
| WAL of the build | 4.6 MB | 0.4 MB | 0.4 MB |

The sidecar is 44 pages (360,448 B) of the segment's stream. The file grows by those, the header page of the segment and the pages that the directory and the free list take; the pages the entity build left free are used first, which is why 64 MB adds four pages fewer than 8 MB.

Other fixtures, in the same terms (bytes added, as a share of the bytes the 5.0 scope segments took for the same data, `maintenance_work_mem` 8 MB; the 64 MB figures are given where they differ):

| fixture | 5.0 scope segments | 5.1 sidecar | share | temporary bytes | instructions of the scope part |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|

**The sidecar of a text, varchar or uuid member is larger than a tenth of the 5.0 bytes** (11.1% to 12.3% on this fixture) because of its month cubes: the units do not hold the members in order, so a key's units are found by the strings of the member leaf and a run of a string member lists every unit of its key, one unit included. All 51 keys have runs (61 of 3.3 units on average; the integer fixture has 35 of 5.0 units for its 27 keys with runs, the others fill one unit and are read by reference) and the cells of their months are 1,525 against 875. A coded key (a `uuid` or `text` scope key over an integer member) has the runs and the size of an integer key. With units of 100 rows, where more units hold a key's rows, the share is 10.4% (241 runs of 12.6 units), also a little over a tenth.

The sidecar is put to the pages of its segment as it is made, in the order of its bytes: the cubes, cells and units of a run when the run is closed, the keys and run records of a scope when it ends, then the records of the scopes, the header and the trailer, so no offset is patched afterwards and no temporary file is written. The memory of the build of a segment's sidecar follows `maintenance_work_mem` (scope `col_side_build`, [SIZING](SIZING.md)), not the rows or the size of the sidecar, and each part has its own share: the rows of the run being built, with the month copies and the cube scratch of the run being closed, take five eighths of it (the run is cut earlier than its 16 units or 131,072 rows when the next unit would pass that, by the bytes of a row for the leaves the cube reads: `side_row_bytes`); the bytes of the sidecar wait in one buffer of a quarter of it, so that a sidecar that fits is written to pages taken to the page (a stream of unknown length takes runs of 16 pages and more, and the end of the last one stays free), and one that outgrows the buffer is put to the pages as it is made; the run records of a scope (56 bytes a run and 16 a key) take an eighth of it, and a scope with more runs keeps those of its first keys: the keys after them are read by reference, with the answer of the join. The bound is the bytes, so it follows `maintenance_work_mem`: a key of one run takes 72 bytes, 1,365 keys at 768 kB (98,304 bytes) and 116,508 keys at the default 64 MB. The build says where it stopped (`NOTICE: scope "<name>": the sidecar of a segment keeps the runs of the first N of M keys`, once for each segment, with the bytes of the share and the hint to raise `maintenance_work_mem` and `REINDEX`), and the read of a key after them says `Scope Fallback: no sidecar holds a run of the key`; a larger `maintenance_work_mem` at the next `REINDEX` keeps them. Test `col_side_keys_cap` holds both sides at 768 kB: 1,365 keys have a run each and the build is silent, 1,366 keep 1,365 and the last reads the join's rows with the reason, and at 1,536 kB every key has its run. A text, varchar or uuid member adds the index from a member to its units, at most `maintenance_work_mem` too (past it the segment has no sidecar), so the scope holds twice `maintenance_work_mem` at most. The pairs of the scope and the open segment are the caller's. `test/sql/col_side_memory.sql` reads the bytes of each context at 160,000 to 640,000 rows and holds them to these terms, with no temporary byte added to the build of the same index without the scope, whether the buffer held the sidecar or the pages took it as it was made.

**Writes.** The statement that writes the facts costs the same with and without the scope: the rows wait in the write buffer, and a promotion writes entity rows, no copy of a row for each key and no sidecar; the next step of the maintenance writes the sidecar of a segment that has 16 units or more. A pair edit costs its journal record, 32 bytes for each pair an UPDATE or DELETE ends and each pair inserted: 100 pairs ended by one UPDATE took 18 to 21 M instructions and 40 to 46 KB of WAL with the journal index, 13 M and 16 KB without it; 100 pairs inserted 6 to 7 M and 35 KB, against 7 M and 14 KB (9,600,000 rows, measured with 5.0; the journal is the same). That is about 220 bytes of WAL for each pair edit over the 32-byte record, which includes the pages of the journal. A pair edit rewrites nothing: the runs it lies in are read by reference until the sidecar is made again.

**How each write keeps the sidecar current.** Only a build and the merge step of the maintenance write a sidecar; every other write leaves the runs as they are and the read says which it may take: a run needs the journal to name no changed member in its span, its units clean (no dead row) and on all-visible pages.

| write | the sidecar entry | until the merge step, a read |
|---|---|---|
| `INSERT` | none: the row waits in the write buffer | takes the runs of the segments, reads the buffer by reference |
| promotion of the buffer | none: the segment is written without one; the next merge step writes it when the segment has 16 units or more | reads it by reference (a segment of fewer than 16 units never has one) |
| merge, roll or rewrite of partitions | none: the output is written without one; the last step of a merge call writes it (`sift.col_merge`, `VACUUM`, the next visit of the worker) | reads the output by reference |
| `DELETE`, and the old version of an `UPDATE` | none: the deletion record or the heap page takes its units out of the all-visible set | reads those runs by their units |
| `VACUUM` | none for the rows it ends: their units are dirty, which a run does not stand for; a segment with net pre-aggregates is made a sidecar again with the net kept. When more than `sift.col_scope_refresh_pct` percent of the units of a segment with a sidecar are dirty, the merge step of the same `VACUUM` rewrites the segment and writes the sidecar of its output | takes the runs of clean units and reads the dirty units by reference until that rewrite, `sift.col_merge(.., true)` or `REINDEX` |
| pair edit | none: the journal records it | takes the runs whose span holds no changed member; the merge step writes the sidecar again once more than `sift.col_scope_refresh_pct` percent of the rows of its runs are in changed runs |
| `CREATE INDEX`, `REINDEX` (also `CONCURRENTLY`) | written at the end of the build (the validation pass of a concurrent one) | takes every run |

**Building at the same time.** The build of a `sift_col` index that tracks changes installs the two triggers of its log on the table when it has read it, which takes `ShareRowExclusiveLock` (for a few milliseconds in a concurrent build, after waiting for the writers that hold the table) while `CREATE INDEX` holds `ShareLock`: two such builds on one table that ran together (`pg_restore -j`) each waited for the other. An event trigger before `CREATE INDEX` of a `sift_col` index
(`sift_col_build_lock`) takes the stronger lock first, so the second build waits at its start; the options a build decides are written under the tuple lock of the catalog row of its index. `REINDEX`
takes no stronger lock than its own `ShareLock` (PostgreSQL has no event trigger for it): it keeps the two triggers of its log when they are what the option made; one that has to make them (disabled, or none yet) asks for `ShareRowExclusiveLock` only when no other statement holds a lock that conflicts with it, since a library that is loaded on first use has no hook before the statement takes `ShareLock`, and two that wait for each other's `ShareLock` deadlock: the one that finds the table held leaves the triggers with a `NOTICE`, the maintenance worker makes missing triggers at the next read that checks the heap (`ALTER INDEX .. SET (track_changes = 'off')`, then the key, makes any other). The rules of a build read the other indexes of the table without a lock on them (a `REINDEX` of one holds `AccessExclusiveLock` on it), so two `REINDEX` of two indexes of one table run together. The event triggers cost a statement of DDL: 10,000 statements (`CREATE TABLE`, `CREATE INDEX`, `DROP INDEX`, `DROP TABLE` in a row, one backend, median of five runs) took 0.94 ms for each four with none, 1.20 with the build lock, 1.26 with the drop sweep and 1.45 with the four event triggers of the library: 0.13 ms for each statement, 1.3 s for the 10,000.

**Workers.** A scope read runs under the `Gather` of a plan that has workers, as an entity read does: the leader decides the read once (the keys, the members listed from its snapshot, and whether the node runs the join as written in the place of its spec), puts that decision in the shared memory of the statement, and each participant adopts it (a worker asks the pairs and the journal nothing). The join a scope read falls back to runs in the participants as the plan holds it. The answers are those of the read in one process and of the join (test `col_scope_workers`).

## Verifying a scope index

    SELECT * FROM sift.col_scope_verify('events_col');                  -- 10 random keys; no row: consistent
    SELECT * FROM sift.col_scope_verify('events_col', 50);              -- 50 keys
    SELECT * FROM sift.col_scope_verify('events_col', keys => ARRAY['7']); -- the key named

`sift.col_scope_verify(index, sample, keys)` runs, for each key and each scope of the index, the statement the planner recognises (`f.fk IN (SELECT member FROM rel WHERE key = k AND <predicate>)`, or the union of the two paths) twice in the
snapshot of the caller: through the scope read (`sift.enable_agg` on, `sift.agg_force`) and through the plain plan (`sift.enable_agg` off, the executor of PostgreSQL alone). Each side answers the number of rows and a signature of the first
column of the index for each member, and the function reports the keys whose two answers differ: `problem = 'differs'`, `scope_rows` and `join_rows` (the rows of each side) and `detail` (how many members differ and the first). The keys are
drawn at random among those that have a pair now (`sample`, default 10), or are the ones named by `keys` (text, cast to the type of the key: integer, text, varchar or uuid), which may also name a key with no pair: the check that the units hold nothing for a key whose pairs all went. A key whose answer was not
read by the scope (a `Scope Fallback` says its reason: the join ran) has `problem = 'unverified'` and the reason in `detail`: nothing was compared for it. No row means every
sampled key was read by the scope and equals the join. It reads each sampled key twice and a third time to learn whether the scope was used, and the plain side is the join of the key's members, so run it on a few keys of a large table;
it changes no setting of the session and no data, and runs with the privileges of the caller. In `REPEATABLE READ` it checks the snapshot of the transaction, on a standby the replayed state (a read-only session). `sift.col_verify(index, true)` checks the structure of the segments against the heap.

**Replication and upgrade.** A physical standby replays the index and reads it (`test/t/col_scope_replica.sh`). The facts and the pairs are heap tables, so a logical subscription carries them: a subscriber that has its own scope index (the facts, the pairs and their journals made there before the subscription copies the rows) keeps it as the apply worker writes the rows, since the journal of the pairs is kept by row triggers that fire for the apply worker, and its reads are the publisher's join; the index itself is not replicated. The oracle gate (`test/t/col_scope_oracle.sh`) compares every read of the primary, a standby, such a subscriber and a restored dump with a node that never loads pg_sift. `pg_upgrade` carries the index with its options and its dependencies on the btrees; the journals and then the scope index are rebuilt after it (`test/t/pg_upgrade.sh`).

## Edges

The expected case needs nothing of what follows. The edges, each with what happens there:

- [where the gain is small](#where-the-gain-is-small): small keys, the requests the runs do not answer, changed pairs, latest-N and top-N, small tables, members in many keys;
- the shapes of pair tables the declaration also takes: [soft deleted pairs](#pairs-that-are-soft-deleted-a-partial-unique-btree), [a pair in several rows](#pairs-in-several-rows-a-multiset), [two paths](#pairs-reached-by-two-paths-a-direct-grant-and-a-group-grant) (two hops: a grant on a member or on a group);
- key types, partitioned tables and the other [limits](#limits).

### Where the gain is small

The sidecar helps a read of a key whose members fill runs, with a request a run answers from its summary. What differs is the key and the request.

- **A small key.** A key whose members fill fewer than two units of a segment has no run (a run of one unit costs what reading the unit does), and a segment of fewer than 16 units has no sidecar: those rows are read by reference. On the lab fixture the small key 50 costs 2.07 M instructions for the sum by kind with or without the runs, and the join 1.29 M: the node takes the join (`no sidecar holds a run of the key`: a key of one unit has no run).
- **The requests the runs do not answer.** A filter on a column, a point lookup of one member, `rollup_columns`, the id sets of `count(DISTINCT member)` by month, a range of time that cuts a calendar month, a cube (a month of a run, or all its rows) whose groups of the dim stand for fewer than 8 rows on average (a dim of 7 groups needs 56 rows: a key of 54 rows has its run with no histogram of the kind, and its read takes the run and reads its units; `col_side_keys_cap`), a time zone other than UTC, the minimum and maximum by a dim over a range of months and a column no cube names read the units of the key by reference, at the cost of [the table without a scope](#without-a-scope-index-reuse) for the units the runs do not stand for. The key groups of the 5.0 scope segments answered the rollup of the rows and the id sets by month; the sidecar keeps neither.
- **Changed pairs.** A key whose members changed reads the units of the runs the changes lie in by reference until the sidecar is made again ([changed pairs](#what-the-planner-recognises)); when the changed members hold much of the key's facts that is most of the key, which the node prices against the join.
- **Latest-N and top-N.** Through the executor the entity arm reads a latest 20 with the generic top-N of the scan: a page with no filter reads the ends of the few units whose zone can beat the page, so it costs 2.4 to 2.8 times the instructions of the 5.0 scope segments on the lab fixture (keys of 13 and 81 units); a filter that passes 10%, 1% or 0.1% of the rows reads every unit that holds a row of the key, about 100,000 instructions a unit (the filter leaf, then the time of the rows that pass), because the entity segments order the rows by (member, time) and nearly every unit spans the whole time range: the newest rows of a key lie in the last rows of each member's run in every unit, where the 5.0 copy was sorted by time and a page lay in one unit. The largest key (81 units) costs 6.2, 5.8 and 3.5 times the 5.0 read at 10%, 1% and 0.1%, a key of 13 units 2.5, 2.1 and 1.4 times (2.6, 2.2 and 1.5 for 16 units), a key of 2 units 1.0 to 1.2 times. The join costs 100 times more (157 to 583 M instructions on the same keys) and the planner never takes it for a key that has runs. What was tried to close it, on the largest key at a 1% filter (251,607 rows examined and 70 units opened by the scan, 25 instructions a row, 90,000 a unit): (1) the order array of the time leaf is grouped by member (a second order), so a walk merges one group for each member; the walk refuses a member list of more than 16 values (`SIFT_GORD_PIN_MAX`) and more than 64 entries for each row of the page (1,280, where a 1% filter needs about 2,000), and opening a group costs about 35,000 instructions (0.52 M for 3 members, 0.97 M for 16, with no filter), so 75 members cost 2.6 M before a filter is tested; (2) lists of the newest rows in the sidecar, 1,024 deep, with the filter leaf read for the listed rows of each unit at once, answer the page from the lists but open 67 units at 55,000 instructions each (4.2 to 4.6 M at the source). Neither reaches 3 times the 5.0 read; lists would with the filter read of the scan (18,000 instructions a unit, about 2.5 to 3 M in all) and a depth of a hundredth of a run's rows, and they cannot reach a 0.1% filter (20,000 rows of the key). Lists of 64 rows a run answered a filter that passes a tenth of the rows at the source (2.3 M against 7.8 M) and cost 3.0% of the 5.0 bytes on the fixture of 100-row units, and no statement path read them: the sidecar keeps none.
- **Small tables.** Below 16 units a segment has no sidecar and the read is the by-reference read of [the table without a scope](#without-a-scope-index-reuse); the saving of a scope index at that size is small.
- **Members in many keys.** A member may be in any number of keys: nothing is copied, so no scope is refused for the entries a row has (5.0 refused above 3 entries a row). A read of several keys that counts a row for each pair runs the join. Leave a scope undeclared when the size, the build and the upkeep of the sidecar are not worth the saving.

### Pairs that are soft deleted: a partial unique btree

The pairs may be the rows of a PARTIAL btree of the relation: the predicate says which rows are pairs, and the rows that satisfy it are unique on the two columns (a partial btree that is not unique makes
the pairs [a multiset](#pairs-in-several-rows-a-multiset) of the rows that satisfy it). A bridge table that soft
deletes keeps its all-rows unique btree (the primary key) and adds the one that defines the pairs:

    CREATE UNIQUE INDEX memberships_active ON memberships (dim_a_id, group_id) WHERE deleted_at IS NULL;
    CREATE INDEX memberships_journal ON memberships USING sift_col (dim_a_id, group_id) WITH (track_changes = 'ctid');
    CREATE INDEX events_col ON events USING sift_col (id, dim_a_id, created_at, kind, value_num)
      WITH (scope = 'group_id: dim_a_id -> memberships(dim_a_id, group_id)');

No table exists for the index alone, and the application deletes as it does. An `UPDATE` that sets `deleted_at` ends a pair and one that clears it creates one: the journal records the member of both,
so a read takes the runs of the members that did not change and reads the units of those that did by reference. A statement that updates a column no index holds is a HOT update and writes
nothing to the journal. The pairs are the active ones only (soft deleted rows are none), and the answer is the join on the active rows.

- **Which btree.** `using <index>` after the columns names it (`memberships(dim_a_id, group_id) using memberships_active`); without it, among the unique btrees on exactly the two columns if there is one, else among the
  non-unique ones, the only partial one, else the lowest oid of the plain ones; two partial ones of that kind are an error that asks for `using`. The `NOTICE` of `CREATE INDEX` and `REINDEX` names it and its predicate; the index records it (the dependency), every read uses it, and one made
  later changes nothing (a partial btree made after a plain one is not taken by a `REINDEX`: a new `CREATE INDEX` chooses again, or `using` names it then). `ALTER INDEX` with another `using` than the recorded btree is refused: the segments were written under the recorded one.
- **Journal index.** A plain one, or a partial one whose predicate the predicate of the pairs' btree implies (`WHERE deleted_at IS NULL` beside the same predicate: a soft delete writes one record). A partial one
  that a pair can be inserted outside of does not qualify: it never hears of that pair.
- **What the predicate can be.** What PostgreSQL accepts in an index predicate: immutable, over the columns of the row (`IS NULL`, comparisons with constants, boolean columns). `now()` is refused by PostgreSQL
  (`functions in index predicate must be marked IMMUTABLE`), so a pair that ends by the clock needs a column the application or a scheduled `UPDATE` sets (`UPDATE grants SET expired = true WHERE expired_at <= now() AND NOT expired`,
  a journaled write: exact as of the sweep, so the staleness is the sweep interval), or the pairs kept from a view ([INDEX-RECIPES](INDEX-RECIPES.md#keeping-the-pairs-from-a-view), which has `sift.scope_expire_pairs`).

### Pairs in several rows: a multiset

A table that keeps a row for each source of a pair (a grant for each way a tenant got a dim_a_id, a row for each role that put a dim_a_id in a group) holds a pair in several rows, and its application does not keep a helper
table only for the index. Declare the scope on a btree that is not unique, plain or partial, on exactly the two columns, in either order:

    CREATE TABLE tenant_pairs (id serial PRIMARY KEY, row_src int NOT NULL, dim_a_id int NOT NULL, tenant_id int NOT NULL);
    CREATE INDEX tenant_pairs_pair ON tenant_pairs (dim_a_id, tenant_id);
    CREATE INDEX tenant_pairs_journal ON tenant_pairs USING sift_col (dim_a_id, tenant_id) WITH (track_changes = 'ctid');
    CREATE INDEX events_col ON events USING sift_col (id, dim_a_id, created_at, kind, value_num)
      WITH (scope = 'tenant_id: dim_a_id -> tenant_pairs(dim_a_id, tenant_id)');
    NOTICE:  scope "tenant_id": the pairs of "tenant_pairs" (dim_a_id, tenant_id) are the distinct rows of its non-unique btree "tenant_pairs_pair"
    DETAIL:  A pair may be several rows: a read with the meaning of IN or EXISTS counts a fact once, a join counts it once for each row and runs as written.

A pair exists while at least one visible row that satisfies the predicate of the btree (if it has one) holds it: the PAIRS are the distinct `(dim_a_id, tenant_id)` of those rows. Deleting one of two rows keeps the pair, deleting the
last one ends it, a soft delete (a partial btree `WHERE deleted_at IS NULL`) of one of two rows keeps it and of both ends it, and moving the key of one row adds the new pair and keeps the old one while another row holds it. When
the relation has several btrees on the two columns the choice is: a unique one before a non-unique one, among those the only partial one, else the lowest oid of the plain ones (two partial ones of that kind are an error that asks
for `using`); `using <index>` names the one, and the `NOTICE` says which.

- **Exact because the journal names at least every changed dim_a_id.** The journal index records the dim_a_id of every row an `UPDATE` or `DELETE` ended and of every row inserted, whether or not the set of distinct pairs of that dim_a_id changed. A read
  takes the runs of the dim_a_id that did not change and reads the units of those that did by reference, listing their pairs under the snapshot of the statement. A duplicate row inserted or deleted
  therefore costs the reading of the units of its dim_a_id, and a pair that did not change is read again from the btree: never a wrong answer. Nothing counts the rows of a pair when it is written (a refinement that skipped the record when
  another live row exists would be wrong under concurrency: two sessions that each delete one of two rows).
- **Every list of the pairs lists each once**: the pairs a sidecar is made from, the member list of a read (the btree probed by key, the leaves of the journal index, the relation read once) and the union of the two paths of a scope of two paths ([where a pair in both, or in several rows of each, is one pair](#pairs-reached-by-two-paths-a-direct-grant-and-a-group-grant)). A pair taken twice would count a fact twice. Tests `col_scope_pairs_multiset` (every state by every read shape), `col_scope_pairs_multiset_partial`, `col_scope_pairs_multiset_twohop`.
- **Which forms are exact.** A read with the meaning of `IN` or `EXISTS` counts a fact once whatever the rows of the pair and is read from the units. A `JOIN` to the relation counts a fact once for each row of a pair, and a read
  for each pair (`GROUP BY` the scope key, several keys counted for each pair) once for each row too, which the units (one entry for each distinct pair) cannot: those run as written, the plan says why
  (`sift.explain_why`, arm `scope`: `pairs are a multiset (non-unique btree): a join counts a fact once for each row`), and their rows are the join's.
  The planner learns it from the btree the index records (a dependency of the index, so a btree made later changes nothing, not even for a `REINDEX`). A scope of two paths defines its pairs as the distinct union, so a read for each pair of it counts each pair once whatever the rows hold.
- **The pairs kept by `sift.scope_follow_pairs`** are written by sift as the distinct rows of a view, so they are unique.
- **What it costs.** The same as for unique pairs: the sidecar summarises the rows of each distinct pair's members once, and a run whose members were not written to the relation since the stamp is taken. A duplicate insert or delete (the test: 1.13 rows a pair) makes the runs of the dim_a_id it touches be read by reference like any other write to the relation.

### Pairs reached by two paths: a direct grant and a group grant

A tenant sees an entity when it has a grant on the entity or on a group the entity belongs to (a subscription). The pairs are not the rows of one relation but the union of two paths, and a
member can be reached by both. The declaration names the three relations and no table holds the union:

    facts(id, dim_a_id, ..)
    grants(id, tenant_id, dim_a_id, group_id, deleted_at, ..)     -- a grant names a member or a group; both columns may be NULL
    memberships(id, group_id, dim_a_id, deleted_at, ..)
    CREATE UNIQUE INDEX grants_direct  ON grants (dim_a_id, tenant_id) WHERE deleted_at IS NULL;
    CREATE UNIQUE INDEX grants_group   ON grants (group_id, tenant_id) WHERE deleted_at IS NULL;
    CREATE UNIQUE INDEX memberships_active ON memberships (group_id, dim_a_id) WHERE deleted_at IS NULL;
    CREATE INDEX grants_by_member         ON grants      USING sift_col (dim_a_id, tenant_id) WITH (track_changes = 'ctid');
    CREATE INDEX grants_by_group          ON grants      USING sift_col (group_id, tenant_id) WITH (track_changes = 'ctid');
    CREATE INDEX memberships_by_member    ON memberships USING sift_col (dim_a_id, group_id) WITH (track_changes = 'ctid');
    CREATE INDEX facts_col ON facts USING sift_col (id, dim_a_id, created_at, kind, value_num)
      WITH (scope = 'tenant_id: dim_a_id -> grants(dim_a_id, tenant_id) | grants(group_id, tenant_id) via memberships(group_id, dim_a_id)');

**The grammar.** One alternative after the `->`: `key: fk -> rel(member, key) [using ix] | rel2(via, key) [using ix2] via vrel(via, member) [using ix3] [cols(a, b)]` (the `cols(..)` is ignored, [as above](#the-declaration)). The first relation is
the direct path (the rows of `rel` are pairs), `rel2` the joined path (a row of `rel2` gives its key to every member of its `via` value), and `vrel` lists the members of a `via` value. A text
that names a second path without `via` and its relation, or a relation with one column, is refused with what is missing. The three sets of columns must differ (`grants(dim_a_id, tenant_id)` twice,
or reversed, is refused), the columns of one role are of one integer type (`via` in `rel2` and `vrel`, `member` in `rel` and `vrel`, `key` in `rel` and `rel2`), and a relation is a heap table that
is not the indexed table. The columns of a scope of two paths may be nullable (a grant on a group has no member, a grant on a member no group): a row with a NULL in a column of its path is not a
pair of that path, as a join drops it; the columns of a scope of one path are still NOT NULL.

**The three btrees.** Each relation has its own recorded btree on exactly its two columns, unique or not, with the rule of the pairs of one relation (above): `CREATE INDEX` chooses it (a unique one first,
then the only partial one, else the lowest oid of the plain ones; two partial ones are an error that asks for `using`) and `REINDEX` keeps it, and both say which, one `NOTICE` for each path (`the direct pairs of "grants" (dim_a_id,
tenant_id) are the rows of its unique btree "grants_direct" that satisfy (deleted_at IS NULL)`, then `the joined pairs` and `the via members`); `ALTER INDEX` is sticky (another `using` than the
recorded one is refused); `DROP INDEX` of a recorded btree is refused without `CASCADE`, and `REINDEX .. CONCURRENTLY` of it keeps the dependency. The dependencies are told apart by the relation
and its two columns, so one scope index depends on up to three btrees of the same relation or of several, and an index may hold a scope of two paths beside scopes of one. A scope of one path declared as before is unchanged.
The relations of a scope of two paths may hold a pair in several rows as well (a grant for each source of it, a row of the via relation for each source of a membership): the pairs are the DISTINCT union either way.

**The pair set.** The pairs of the scope are the DISTINCT `(member, key)` of the direct rows D and of the joined ones J, `J = { (m.member, g.key) : g in rel2, m in vrel, m.via = g.via }`, the rows of each
that satisfy the predicate of its btree. The SQL that says it:

    SELECT dim_a_id AS member, tenant_id FROM grants WHERE deleted_at IS NULL AND dim_a_id IS NOT NULL
    UNION
    SELECT m.dim_a_id, g.tenant_id FROM grants g JOIN memberships m ON m.group_id = g.group_id
     WHERE g.deleted_at IS NULL AND m.deleted_at IS NULL;

The tests compare every read with a join on this statement after every edit of the three tables.

**Once and per pair.** A member reached directly and through one or several groups is ONE pair, and no fact is counted twice for a key. A read with the meaning of `IN` or `EXISTS` counts a fact once; a join or `GROUP BY` the key over the `UNION` counts it once for each pair, which is once for a key a member has by any number of paths (`Scope Semantics: once`, `per pair`, as for one path).

**The three journals.** The pairs of a member depend on (1) the rows of `rel` with that member, (2) its rows of `vrel`, and (3) the rows of `rel2` of every group it is in. Each relation needs a
journal index, a `sift_col` index with `track_changes = 'ctid'` whose first column is the journaled key and that holds the other column, plain or partial with a predicate that the predicate of the
recorded btree implies (the rule of the journal index above): J1 on `rel` is `(member, key)`, J2 on `rel2` is `(via, key)`, J3 on `vrel` is `(member, via)`. `CREATE INDEX` of a scope of two paths, as of one path,
makes the missing ones (a role that does not own a relation without one is refused, and the hint has the `CREATE INDEX` for its owner). The scope index finds the journals when it is created or rebuilt and depends on them (a journal made later is found by
the next `REINDEX` of the scope index, which starts it before it reads and stamps the sidecars with it). Each journal has its own stamp (test `col_scope_journal_reindex`).

**What a read of two paths does.** A scope of two paths has the sidecar of one path. Its runs summarise the rows of the members of the distinct union of the two paths (the pairs `sift_scope_all_pairs` lists, each once whatever the relations hold twice), and the record of the scope holds the three stamps, one for each journal. A run is taken when none of the members within its span changed since the stamps: the members of the journals of the direct grants and of the memberships, and every member the memberships list for a group of the journal of the grants of groups (a member of a changed group is a changed member, so that a group granted to a key, revoked from it, or a member added to it, moved or removed, takes the runs of the members of that group back to their units); more than `SIDE_CHANGED_CAP` (65,536) members, or a journal that does not vouch for its stamp, take no run of the segment. A member that has the pair by both paths and loses one of them is read as changed, and its run is read by its units. The rows are the join's in every state (tests `col_scope_twohop_side`, `col_scope_twohop_side_multiset`, `col_scope_texttwohop_side`, `col_side_fuzz_twohop`). 5.0 wrote the segments of such an index at column format minor 49 ([FORMATS.md](FORMATS.md)).

**The planner and the legs.** The scan takes the conjunct from the join as an application writes it (the recognition below), as for one path. The planner reads the paths from `sift_scope_legs(index, scope, legs[2])` (2 legs for this declaration, the second one joined: `via_rel`, `via_join`, `via_member`, `via_btree`, `via_pred`) and recognises the `UNION` of the two
paths as the sub-select of `IN` or `EXISTS` and as the relation of a join (table below): each select reads the relation of its path with the predicate of its btree, both bind the same key by constants or parameters, and no other condition or column
is read. What else a statement says keeps the join as written, and `sift.explain_why` (arm `scope`) says which select breaks the proof.

**What stays impossible.** A relation that is a view, a CTE or a function (the three are heap tables with a btree on their two columns, unique or not); a condition on a third table (a group's own soft delete: the memberships of a deleted group
stay pairs; put the condition on the memberships or keep the pairs from a view with [INDEX-RECIPES](INDEX-RECIPES.md#keeping-the-pairs-from-a-view)); a predicate that depends on time (`now()` is refused in an index predicate: sweep it
into a column, see above); a partitioned table among the three; deny rows (a row that removes a pair another path gives); three or more hops, or more than one joined path.

### Key types

The scope key may be a `text`, `varchar` or `uuid` column of the pairs relation (a deterministic collation: equal values have equal bytes; `char(n)`, `citext` and nondeterministic collations are refused with their reason). The planner takes the same forms as for an integer key (`=`, `IN`, `ANY`, a parameter, a table of keys); an equality under a nondeterministic collation keeps the join as written. The sidecar of such a scope is keyed by the code the key has in the value store of its column of the pairs: `CREATE INDEX`, `REINDEX` and the merge append the keys the store lacks as one run of the store, in a memory of 16 bytes for each pair and the distinct key strings twice, and a read looks the key up in the store without adding it. The codes of a scope index do not move (the store epoch is pinned while the index exists, and `ALTER INDEX` cannot change the scope of a built index), the runs compare the member keys of the pairs as for an integer key, and a pair of a key that changed is found by the journal as ever. A key the store lacks (a key that was never in a pair at the last build) has no run and runs the join, and the rows are the join's for every spelling of a key: a case, a trailing space and an accent are other keys, as in the pairs (tests `col_scope_codedkey`, `col_scope_codedkey_collide`, `col_side_fuzz_coded`, `col_scope_textkey_fuzz`). The latest rows of a key (`ORDER BY` the time column `DESC LIMIT n`) are the top-N of the entity rows, as for an integer key, whether the key is a constant or a parameter: 3.0 M instructions at the largest key of the F2 fixture against 1.0 M for 5.0 and 658 M for the join, 1.2 M against 1.05 M at a key of two units (`col_scope_codedkey`). A text, varchar or uuid member has a sidecar of its own kind ([member types](#member-types)).

### Member types

The member may be a `text`, `varchar` or `uuid` column too, on both sides: the column of the indexed table and the member of the pairs relation are of one kind (text with varchar, uuid with uuid, an integer with an integer), under a deterministic collation, so that equal values have equal bytes (`char(n)`, whose values are equal when they differ in blank padding, `citext`, a domain and a nondeterministic collation are refused at the declaration, with the column and the fix). The journal of the pairs relation, which a reader asks for the members that changed, records for a member the 64-bit word of its bytes: FNV-1a (offset basis 14695981039346656037, prime 1099511628211, over the bytes of the text, or the 16 bytes of the uuid), a fixed function with no seed, so the records a standby reads or a restore brings back are the ones written (the test `col_scope_textmember` pins `''`, `'a'`, `'foobar'` and two uuids to their values, which an arm64 build must give too). The word is a filter, not an identity: a member whose word is another's is read as changed, which can only widen what is re-read. A `NULL` member of a fact has no pair, as for an integer; the empty string is a member like another. **A read of a scope with a text, varchar or uuid member reads the entity rows by value.** The members the pairs list for the keys are strings of a map of the statement (a code for each distinct value), and the member conjunct is `member = ANY(strings)` on the member leaf, tested against the dictionary of each unit (its own for a unit that stores its strings inline), not row by row. The sidecar of such a scope has no span to say which members a run stands for, since the journal knows a member only by its word, which orders nothing: the build finds the units that hold a member of the key from one pass over the units (8 bytes for each member of each unit, at most `maintenance_work_mem`: a segment that needs more has no sidecar and is read by reference), and a run keeps a Bloom filter (about 10 bits for each distinct member of its units, at most 4 bits for each row of them, 7 hashes) over the words of every member its units hold, the key's or not; the filter is of the units, so the runs of the keys over the same units share one. A run is taken when no word of the members that changed since the stamp is in its filter (a word in it by chance, 1% for a changed member, only makes the run give up), and the other conditions of a run hold. The runs are cut by the same rules as those of an integer member; a scan never splits one, because the items of a scan end where no run of its key is cut (`sift_col_super_align`). The runs of a key list every unit of the segment that holds one of its members (a run of one unit too), so a segment whose sidecar no listed member's pair has changed since (the journal's words against the words of the listed members) is read by its runs and by no other unit: the units outside them hold none of the members. A listed member that was added or removed since makes the segment read every unit the member conjunct leaves, as a segment with no sidecar does. The units of a run that cannot be taken are read by reference with the members the read lists, which are exact. The build reads the pairs as committed, without the changes of its own transaction, because the journal that vouches for the runs forgets a transaction that rolls back. Tests: `col_scope_codedmember`, `col_scope_codedmember_collide` (the word cut to 3 bits), `col_scope_textmember`, `col_side_fuzz_coded`.

### Two paths with text, varchar or uuid keys and members

A scope of two paths (a direct grant and a grant on a group) takes a `text`, `varchar` or `uuid` scope key and member too, with the same refusals as one path. The scope key of the direct pairs and of the grants of groups are of one type, and the member of the direct pairs and of the memberships are of one type (a declaration where they differ is refused with both columns and their types); the group column that joins the second path to the memberships stays an `int2`, `int4` or `int8`. The three journals record a member as the journal of one path does (the word of its bytes) and a group by its integer, and tag a record by the word of its key cut to 14 bits, as an integer key tags it by its value (test `col_scope_textkey_tags`; a record with no tag is of every key). The reads run the join. Test `col_scope_textkey_refusals`.

### Limits

A library before 5.1 refuses an index that has a sidecar, and one before 5.0 refuses the option. What a scope does not do in 5.1:

- **Declaration.** The member key and the columns of the relations are `int2`, `int4` or `int8`, or `text`, `varchar` or `uuid` of a deterministic collation, in a scope of one path or of two (see [Key types](#key-types), [Member types](#member-types) and [Two paths with text](#two-paths-with-text-varchar-or-uuid-keys-and-members); the group column of a scope of two paths stays an integer; `char(n)`, `citext`, a domain and a nondeterministic collation are refused with their reason, and the member of the pairs and the column of the index are of one kind). A relation is a heap table that is not partitioned, not a view and not the indexed
  table; a scope of one path has both columns NOT NULL. At most 4 scopes and 8 connections on an index, and a scan reads one scope. At most one joined path and no third hop; no deny rows; no condition on a third table and no predicate that depends on time.
- **The sidecar.** One is made for each scope of an index (at most 4; one path or two) whose pairs have the journals it needs (one, or three) when the index has a `date`, `timestamp` or `timestamptz` column; for each entity segment of 16 units or more. An integer member has runs when the units of the segment hold the members in order (the member is the first column of the order of the index); a scope whose member is not in that order has a record with no run in that segment and its reads run the join, and so do those of a segment of fewer than 16 units; a `text`, `varchar` or `uuid` member needs the index from its members to their units to fit `maintenance_work_mem`. Calendar months are UTC; a time zone other than UTC reads the units.
- **Forms.** A statement that is not in the table of recognised forms keeps the join (`sift.explain_why`, arm `scope`): among them a join or a read for each pair over the pairs of a non-unique btree, a join to a table of keys that may hold a key twice, a key in an expression, a range of keys, `ROLLUP` / `CUBE` / grouping sets over the key.
- **Reads that run the join.** A group by the scope key, several keys that count a row for each pair ([What a read does](#what-a-read-does) and the planner's **Reads that always run the join**).
- **What a run does not answer.** The rollup of the rows, id sets by month, a filter on a column, a point lookup, a range that cuts a month and the aggregates of a leaf no cube names read the units by reference ([the edges](#where-the-gain-is-small)). A latest-N read takes no run: the executor reads it with the generic top-N of the scan.
- **An index built by 5.0.** Its scope segments are not read: its scope reads run the join and `EXPLAIN` says `Scope Fallback: scope segments of an older format (REINDEX)` until `REINDEX` writes the sidecar ([upgrading](UPGRADING.md#what-51-changes-for-a-scope-index)).
- **Generic plans.** A condition on the relation that reads a parameter is proved only by a custom plan: a generic plan keeps the join for a relation with a partial btree.

## Without a scope index: reuse

Without a scope index (none declared or `sift.agg_scope = off`) the facts of one key are read from the plain `sift_col` index of the table by the member keys of the key, `fact.member IN (SELECT member FROM pairs WHERE key = k)`: **reuse**. The scan takes the keys the pair table lists as a conjunct on the member column of the index (a join key filter, [AGG](AGG.md#join-key-filters-and-shared-lookups): the member column is an integer or date leaf of the index, any `sift_col` index with one). A unit whose zone of the member column holds none of the keys is not read and the other rows are tested as a set, so reuse needs no declaration, no journal and no storage of its own, is exact at the time of the statement, has no limit on the keys of a member, and is chosen by the planner by cost against the plain join (`sift.agg_join_filter = off` restores the plain join). How many units the zones leave depends on the layout of the index: for facts ordered by time most units hold a member of a large key. **Replicate** was the scope index of 5.0, whose scope segments held the rows of each key; 5.1 has no such arm: the runs of the sidecar stand for units of the one copy, and the units no run stands for are read as reuse reads them. With a scope index the statement is planned both ways and the estimated costs choose (what the planner recognises, above); where there is none the member keys are read. The tables are measured with the 5.0 scope segments as the arm that copies the rows; the reuse columns are the cost of reading the key's members by reference in 5.1 too.

Measured with the 5.0 scope segments on a table of `events` (member, time, kind, value, quantity) joined to a table of (member, tenant) pairs, 2.7 entries a row, four tenants of 0.1%, 3.3%, 6.6% and 33% of the events, the six requests of a dashboard
(sum by kind, sum by kind in one month, distinct members by month, top 20 members, a filter on the value, latest 20). Warm: instructions of the backend (geometric mean of the six) and shared buffers hit; cold: blocks read with
every relation of the statement evicted. Reuse is, in each cell, the better of two layouts of the entity index (units cut by time, or by the member and then time). Every pair of cells returned the same rows.

Reuse costs about 6 instructions a row of every unit that holds a member of the key, because the member column is decoded to find them (a profile of the 500,000-event run of the 6.6% tenant), and its member list about 1.9 thousand instructions a member; replicate reads the rows of the key
and answers sums and counts by kind and month from stored combinations. At a fixed share of the tenant both grow with the table: from 100,000 to 2,000,000 events (20 times the rows) reuse grew 4.1 times at the 0.1% tenant and 10.1 times at the 33% one, replicate 2.0 and 6.8 times. Reuse comes to about 25 instructions an event at the 33% tenant and about 5 at the 0.1% one (the 2,000,000-event run over its events). Nothing above 2,000,000 events was measured.

is not an alternative: its cells are 85% of the facts at 25 facts a member over 10 kinds and 12 months, and its writes cost +125% (insert), +73% (update) and +140% (delete) instructions.

## A denormalised table: not shipped

A copy of the fact table for each scope key of its member, kept by triggers and read by a plain query on a table (`WHERE tenant_id = k`), is the other way to make a request by a key above the fact table cost what the key holds. 5.0 does not ship a function that builds one: [the comparison below](#against-tuned-stock-postgresql) builds it by the benchmark's own SQL (`denorm_flat`, one table with a `(tenant, time)` covering b-tree, and `denorm_part`, a list partition by tenant) and finds it 13 to 28 times slower than the scope index to read for the tenants of 6% or more and 3 to 4 times dearer to write; it wins only the latest 20 rows of a key and a selective filter. Facts that must stay one table, with no second copy to size, to vacuum and to keep by triggers on the write path, are the scope index's case; pairs that are a view or depend on time are [kept by `sift.scope_follow_pairs` and `sift.scope_expire_pairs`](INDEX-RECIPES.md#keeping-the-pairs-from-a-view). A table built that way takes the first column of its `sift_col` index as the key of `key_order` and `key_cut` by itself (the build rule for tables of that shape).

## Prior art: what exists and what differs

Reaching facts by a key that they have through a join, and storing or indexing them that way, is an old idea with many implementations. This section sets the scope index beside the ones whose own documentation or paper was read for it, on 2026-11-13 (the pages and versions are listed after the table). No system was run for this section. The first five rows use the figures [measured above](#against-tuned-stock-postgresql) on the stock-arms fixture (one generated fixture, one backend, warm; instructions of the backend, 10,000 inserted events, one member granted to a tenant); for every other row the table compares mechanisms taken from the documentation, with no figure, and a cell the documentation read does not settle says "not verified".

**What is not new.** The idea: store, or index, the facts of a table by a key that they reach through a join, and keep it right as the pairs change. A join index of pairs of tuple surrogates is Valduriez's of 1987, and that paper already lets the update of the index wait until the next join, with a list of the changed tuples and a semi join to reconcile them. Bitmap join indexes, indexed views, materialized views and the copies kept by triggers store the join or an aggregate of it; Leopard flattens group membership into an index and merges the changes made since the index was built at the query's timestamp, which is the same family of mechanism as the runs and the units read by reference here (a summary made at a position of the journal, plus the changes since it, read together under a snapshot). Systems that rewrite the plain query to a stored result are common (Oracle, SQL Server, StarRocks, Doris). A many-to-many relation reached by two paths, direct and through a group, is also the shape of group-based authorization (a direct grant and a grant through a group), the case of the engines derived from Zanzibar.

**What is particular, as far as the documentation read shows.** The combination: the rows are kept once in the index of the fact table itself, with summaries of runs of their units for each scope key, so no second table, view or trigger exists; a pair edit of a many-to-many relation is one small journal record, with no rewrite of the member's facts, and a read reconciles it exactly under the reader's own MVCC snapshot, by taking the runs of the members whose pairs did not change and reading the units of the others by reference; two paths, direct and through a group, are one scope; and the planner recognises the join the application already writes and chooses between the units, the member keys and the join by estimated cost. None of the pages read describes that combination. This is a statement about those pages, not a search of the literature or of closed products, and each part has a counterpart above. The closest in mechanism is Leopard, which indexes membership sets and not the facts that the sets select. The ones that store a join result (the views, the copies, the bitmap join index) pay for a pair change in the maintenance of what they store, as the three measured rows show.

**Where the others are better.** A fitted aggregate: pg_ivm answered 22 of the 64 cells it covers faster than the scope read (count by score 0.05 ms against 3.5 ms at tenant 1), and Oracle, SQL Server, StarRocks and Doris store aggregates of a join of any fitted shape, though only pg_ivm was measured here. A latest-N or a selective filter: a copy kept by triggers answered latest 20 in 0.04 to 0.06 ms against 0.3 to 2.2 ms. The tuned semi join beat the scope read in 5 of 72 cells, and is within 0.5 ms of it for a tenant of 0.1% of the events. Materialized views and indexed views have releases of use and tooling behind them, `ON COMMIT` refresh in Oracle is part of the commit, and ClickHouse computes its aggregates at insert time, with no join at read time (a stored fact keeps the pairs it had when it was inserted, which answers a different question from the join). Leopard serves set algebra over deeply nested groups across shards, which a scope of two paths does not (no third hop, no deny rows); Elasticsearch and OpenSearch add relevance scoring. Its figures are those of generated fixtures of up to 2,000,000 rows and of scopes of one path; the sidecar of a scope of two paths is tested for its rows, not measured.

## Checklist: what a scope needs, and what says so when it is missing
Each line is checked when the index is made (`CREATE INDEX`, `REINDEX`) or altered, in the style of the other messages of the extension: an `ERROR` with a hint that names the fix where the index cannot be made, a `NOTICE` where it is made and some reads run as written. A statement that holds a scope relation and is not read from the units says why in `sift.explain_why` (arm `scope`), for every form, including the ones no site of the planner names (no scan of the table is planned, the scan does not take the join, `sift.agg_scope` or `sift.enable_agg` off, the scope declared after the segments were written).

| What the scope needs | When it is not so | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| The indexed table is a heap table and the index a `sift_col` index; the member key is a column of the index of type `int2`, `int4`, `int8`, `text`, `varchar` or `uuid` (a deterministic collation) | `ERROR`: the index must be a `sift_col` index on a heap table; the column is not of one of those types (`char(n)`, `citext`, a domain, a nondeterministic collation) | index a heap table; use a column of a supported type |
| The name of the derived key is not a column of the index | `ERROR` naming the column | rename the key |
| The relation of pairs is a plain permanent heap table, not the indexed table | `ERROR`: not a plain heap table (a view, a materialized view, a partitioned or foreign table), the table of the index, a temporary table (the index would be dropped with it when the session ends) | name a permanent table of pairs; a view is read by the statements, not declared |
| Its member and scope key columns are of one kind on every path (integers, `text` with `varchar`, or `uuid`; the group column of two paths an integer), `NOT NULL` on a scope of one path | `ERROR`: of an unsupported type, nullable, the same column, kinds that differ | `ALTER TABLE .. SET NOT NULL`; change a column or the declaration |
| An immediate btree on exactly `(member, scope_key)`, in either order | `ERROR`: no btree, or only a deferrable or `INCLUDE` one; two partial ones of one kind (name the one that defines the pairs with `using`) | `CREATE UNIQUE INDEX ON pairs (member, scope_key)`; `using <index>` |
| The journal index: a `sift_col` index of the relation with `track_changes = 'ctid'`, the member first, holding the scope key, valid, without a `WHERE` (unless the btree of the pairs implies it) | `NOTICE`: none qualifies, and the statement made it (named by the rule, commented, dropped with the last scope index that uses it). `ERROR`: the role does not own the relation of pairs, and the hint has the statement for its owner | the statement of the hint, run by the owner; `CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY ON pairs USING sift_col (member, scope_key) WITH (track_changes = 'ctid')` first, to name it or keep the writers of the pairs running |
| A scope of two paths has the journals of its three relations (as a scope of one path has its journal) | as above, for each relation without one; the `ERROR` names the first and the hint has the statement for each | the same |
| The journal stays a journal | `ALTER INDEX journal SET (track_changes = ..)` to another value, or `RESET`, is refused while a scope index depends on it | `DROP INDEX` the scope index first |
| `ALTER INDEX .. SET (scope = ..)`, `SET (scope = 'off')` or `RESET (scope)` on an index that has segments | `ERROR` `0A000` (`cannot change the column definitions of index ".." while it holds data`): the segments were written under the declaration the index had, and every scan of the index would stop until a `REINDEX` writes them | `CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY` the index with the scope (or without it, to give the scope up), then `DROP INDEX` the old one: no scan is lost |
| The scope of a partition is declared on the partition | `NOTICE`: a statement over the partitioned table reads no scope | read the partition, or leave the statement as it is |
| The relation has no row-level security that applies to the reader | `NOTICE`: a role the policies apply to reads the join as written | none needed, or a role the policies do not apply to |
| The role that builds (`CREATE INDEX`, `REINDEX`) can read the pairs: `SELECT` on the relation or on its member, scope key and predicate columns, and no row-level security that applies to it | `ERROR`: `permission denied for column .. of table ..` with the `GRANT` in the hint, or `row-level security applies to ..` | the `GRANT` shown; build with the owner of the relation or a role with `BYPASSRLS` |

`DROP INDEX` of the btree or the journal, and `DROP TABLE` of the relation, need `CASCADE` and drop the scope index with them (the dependencies are the record of which btree and journal the segments were written under).
